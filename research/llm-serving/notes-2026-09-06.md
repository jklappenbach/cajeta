# ML serving & training papers — requirements extraction for the cajeta.xpu kernel scheduler

**Purpose.** Engineering requirements for (a) a single-process, single-GPU **kernel scheduler**
that co-runs complementary kernels, and (b) a **cooperative-tile kernel surface**
(`Group`/`Tile`/`mac`/`reduce`) whose launches cross a submit seam carrying geometry, read/write
buffer sets, an arithmetic-character tag, and a schedule policy (throughput / latency /
frame-budget). Workload class: heavy multimodal ML — LLM prefill and decode, TTS/STT, CNN vision,
MoE, and training.

**Scope note.** These papers are, with one exception, about *request* scheduling and *cluster*
placement. Everything below re-reads them for what they imply **inside one process on one GPU**.
Cluster-placement findings (machine pools, cross-node KV transfer, provisioning) are recorded only
where the mechanism generalizes down to intra-process scheduling. The exception is FlashInfer
(§7), which is literally a kernel scheduler and should be read first.

**Numbers policy.** Every number below is from a named paper with its hardware. Derived numbers
(the roofline algebra in §13.1) are marked as derived, and anything from my own knowledge rather
than these papers — TTS/STT and CNN pipelines, launch-overhead magnitudes — is marked **[not from
these papers]**.

**Read order if you only read three:** §7 FlashInfer (a kernel scheduler), §3 Sarathi-Serve (the
roofline crossover numbers), §13 Synthesis. §5 DistServe is the counter-argument that keeps §3
honest.

**Contents.** 1 Orca · 2 vLLM/PagedAttention · 3 Sarathi-Serve · 4 Splitwise · 5 DistServe ·

## 1. Yu et al. 2022 — Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models (OSDI'22)

### 1.1 Core mechanism

Two ideas. **Iteration-level scheduling**: the serving system invokes the execution engine for
*one iteration* (one token step) at a time instead of running a whole batch of requests to
completion, so finished requests return immediately and newly arrived requests join after one
iteration instead of waiting for the slowest member of a batch. **Selective batching**: because
requests in a batch are at different token indices with different KV lengths, their tensors are
not uniformly shaped, so batching cannot be applied uniformly. Orca flattens the batch into a
single `[ΣL, H]` token-wise tensor and applies batched execution to every parameter-bearing
operation (QKV Linear, Attn Out Linear, MLP Linear, LayerNorm, Add, GeLU) while **splitting the
batch and running Attention per request**, then merging back. Attention has no model parameters,
so not batching it costs almost nothing. A per-request **Attention K/V manager** holds the KV
across iterations, and the scheduler reserves `max_tokens` KV slots per admitted request against
a fixed pool `n_slots`.

### 1.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Hardware: Azure ND96asr A100 v4 VMs, **8 × NVIDIA A100 40 GB with NVLink**, 8 × Mellanox
  200 Gbps HDR InfiniBand, 1.6 Tb/s inter-VM. fp16, max sequence length 2048.
- Models (Table 1): 13B (40 layers, hidden 5120, 1 inter × 1 intra partition), 101B (80, 10240,
  1×8), 175B (96, 12288, 2×8), 341B (120, 15360, 4×8).
- **36.9× throughput at the same latency**: 175B, at a median normalized latency of 190 ms/token,
  FasterTransformer sustains **0.185 req/s** vs Orca **6.81 req/s**.
- Engine microbenchmark (no scheduler, homogeneous batch): Orca is **similar or slightly worse**
  than FasterTransformer, precisely because it does not batch attention. With pipelining disabled
  on the 175B model Orca is **up to 47% faster**, attributed to the control-plane/data-plane
  separation (gRPC for control, NCCL only for tensors).
- FasterTransformer **cannot run batch ≥ 8 on 13B (≥ 16 on 101B)** because it pre-allocates KV
  per request proportional to the 2048 max sequence length — a static-allocation OOM that Orca's
  per-request `max_tokens` sizing avoids.
- Trace: input tokens `U(32, 512)`, `max_gen_tokens` `U(1, 128)`, Poisson arrivals.

### 1.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **The load-bearing sentence for a kernel scheduler** (§5, Implementation): "we go one step
  further and fuse the kernels of the split Attention operators by simply **concatenating all
  thread blocks of the kernels for different requests**. Although this fusion makes the thread
  blocks within a kernel have different characteristics and lifetimes (**which is often
  discouraged by CUDA programming practice**) because they process tensors of different shapes,
  we find this fusion to be beneficial by **improving GPU utilization and reducing the kernel
  launch overhead**." That is a single-GPU, single-process co-scheduling result stated in 2022:
  *heterogeneous work items in one launch beat many homogeneous launches.* It is the direct
  ancestor of FlashInfer's work-queue-of-(tile, chunk) design, and it is the shape the cajeta
  submit seam should support natively — a launch whose work items are non-uniform.
- **The design principle is a roofline statement**: "reading parameters from GPU global memory is
  a major bottleneck in terms of end-to-end execution time, for large-scale models. Adhering to
  this principle, we apply iteration-level scheduling and selective batching to process all
  'ready' tokens in a single round of parameter read." Batching exists to amortize the *weight*
  fetch. That is why batching helps the linear layers (weights reused across tokens) and does
  nothing for attention (no weights; KV traffic scales with the batch).
- **Three enumerated reasons two requests cannot share a batched kernel**, which is exactly the
  legality predicate a scheduler needs: (1) both in prefill with different input lengths;
  (2) both in decode at different token indices (different KV shapes); (3) one in prefill and one
  in decode. Operations "incompatible with such irregularly shaped tensors" are the
  non-attention ones — and they are made to work "by flattening the tensors."
  **So: flattenable ops batch on the token axis; attention does not, and needs a ragged/grouped
  launch.**
- **KV memory is a scheduler-visible admission constraint, not an allocator concern.** Orca's
  scheduler must know `n_slots` and reserve `max_tokens` per request or it deadlocks ("a naïve
  implementation can make the scheduler fall into a deadlock when the scheduler cannot issue an
  iteration for a new request in the pool because there is no space left for storing a new
  Attention key and value"). Any cajeta policy that admits work must model the buffer set's
  *residency*, not just its liveness.
- **Control-plane/data-plane separation removes per-iteration CPU–GPU synchronization.** Orca
  observes that FasterTransformer and Megatron-LM "have CPU-GPU synchronization whenever each
  process receives control messages, because they exchange the messages through a GPU-to-GPU
  communication channel — NCCL. The exchange of these control messages occurs at every
  iteration, imposing a non-negligible performance overhead." For cajeta this argues the submit
  seam's metadata path must never ride the device queue.
- **Pipelining is done by keeping `n_workers` batches in flight** rather than by splitting a
  batch into microbatches — avoiding the batch-efficiency-vs-bubble trade FasterTransformer has.
  The single-process analogue is: keep enough independent launches enqueued to cover the
  scheduler's own latency, rather than shrinking each launch.

### 1.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per work item (not per launch): request id, phase (initiation/increment), current token index,
input token count, KV region handle and length, and `max_tokens` reservation. Per launch:
whether the op is "flattenable on the token axis" (parameter-bearing) or "must be split per
request" (attention); the total flattened token count `ΣL`; and the KV pool occupancy so
admission can be decided.

### 1.5 Testable requirements

1. When a launch's work items have differing shapes, the submit seam must accept a *list* of work
   items with per-item geometry and issue one launch, not one launch per item.
2. When an operation is parameter-bearing, the scheduler must be permitted to merge work from
   multiple logical requests onto the token axis; when it is attention (no parameters, per-request
   KV), it must not.
3. When a new work item is admitted, the scheduler must verify that its declared peak buffer
   reservation fits the remaining residency budget, and must refuse admission rather than fail
   mid-iteration.
4. When scheduling metadata is exchanged, it must not traverse the device data path or force a
   device synchronization.
5. When a batch member completes, its result must be observable after the current iteration
   without waiting for other members of the same launch.
6. When the number of in-flight launches falls below the depth needed to cover scheduler latency,
   the scheduler must enqueue more independent work rather than enlarging the current launch.

## 2. Kwon et al. 2023 — Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention / vLLM (SOSP'23)

### 2.1 Core mechanism

The KV cache is the binding resource in LLM serving, and existing systems stored each request's
KV in one contiguous chunk sized to the model's *maximum* sequence length, so most of it was
never used. **PagedAttention** partitions each sequence's KV into fixed-size **blocks** of `B`
tokens that need not be contiguous in physical memory; a per-request **block table** maps logical
blocks to physical blocks, exactly as an OS page table maps virtual to physical pages. The
attention kernel is rewritten to fetch `K_j`, `V_j` block by block through the table and combine
per-block partial attention. Blocks are reference-counted, so a shared prompt prefix, the samples
of a parallel-sampling request, or the surviving candidates of a beam search physically share
blocks, with **copy-on-write at block granularity**. A centralized scheduler owns the block
tables and drives all workers; when memory runs out it preempts whole sequence groups
(**all-or-nothing eviction**, gang-scheduled) and recovers either by **swapping** blocks to CPU
RAM or by **recomputation**.

### 2.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Memory split, 13B model on **A100 40 GB**: parameters **26 GB (65%)**, KV cache **>30%**,
  remainder ephemeral activations. Per-token KV for OPT-13B = **800 KB**
  (`2 × 5120 × 40 × 2 bytes`), so a 2048-token request needs **1.6 GB** of KV.
- Configurations (Table 1): 13B → 1×A100, 26 GB params, 12 GB KV, **15.7K KV slots**;
  66B → 4×A100, 132 GB params, 21 GB KV, **9.7K slots**; 175B → 8×A100-80GB, 346 GB params,
  264 GB KV, **60.1K slots**.
- **Effective KV utilization before vLLM was as low as 20.4%** (Fig. 2): Orca(Max) =
  20.4% token states / 13.3% reservation / **57.3% internal fragmentation** / 8.9% external;
  Orca(Pow2) 26.8 / 17.9 / 13.6 / 41.6; Orca(Oracle) 38.2 / 25.2 / — / 36.6; **vLLM 96.3%**.
- Throughput **2–4×** over FasterTransformer and Orca at equal latency; on ShareGPT,
  **1.7–2.7× vs Orca(Oracle)** and **2.7–8× vs Orca(Max)**; up to **22× vs FasterTransformer**.
- Batched requests actually resident (Fig. 13), OPT-13B: ShareGPT @2 req/s — Orca(Max) 7.00,
  Orca(Pow2) 9.81, Orca(Oracle) 13.62, **vLLM 30.42**; Alpaca @30 req/s — 7.00 / 43.24 / 72.75 /
  **132.44**. *Residency, not compute, set the batch size.*
- Sharing savings: parallel sampling **6.1–9.8%** (Alpaca) / 16.2–30.5% (ShareGPT); beam search
  **37.6–55.2%** (Alpaca) / 44.3–66.3% (ShareGPT). Shared prefix: 1.67× (80-token prefix) and
  **3.58×** (341-token prefix) over Orca(Oracle).
- **The cost of the indirection, measured**: "this leads to **20–26% higher attention kernel
  latency** compared to the highly-optimized FasterTransformer implementation" from block-table
  access, extra branches, and variable-length handling — "the overhead is small as it only
  affects the attention operator but not the other operators in the model, such as Linear."
  Fig. 18a: at context 256, batch 32, ≈250 µs (vLLM) vs ≈200 µs (FT).
- **Block size**: too small under-utilizes GPU parallelism when reading KV; too large increases
  internal fragmentation and kills sharing. ShareGPT best at **16–128**; Alpaca best at **16–32**
  and *significantly degraded* beyond, because sequences are shorter than the block.
  **vLLM's default block size is 16.**
- Recovery: swapping's overhead is excessive at small block sizes (many small PCIe transfers);
  recomputation's overhead is **constant across block sizes and never more than 20% of
  swapping's latency**; for block sizes 16–64 the two are comparable end-to-end.
- Implementation: 8.5K lines Python + **2K lines C++/CUDA**.

### 2.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **KV-cache residency is a scheduler-visible resource with its own admission control**, distinct
  from the transient working set of a launch. It is (a) large — comparable to the weights,
  (b) long-lived across many launches, (c) *growing* during the launch sequence, and (d) shareable
  by reference counting. A cajeta submit seam that only carries read/write *buffer sets* cannot
  express this; it needs a notion of a resident pool with occupancy, per-item reservations, and an
  eviction/recompute policy.
- **Indirection costs a measurable, bounded 20–26% on exactly one kernel.** That is the price of
  making the KV layout dynamic, and it is worth paying because it doubles-to-quadruples the batch.
  This is a scheduler-level trade the *kernel author* cannot make: only something that sees both
  the batch size and the kernel cost can decide. It also says the paged path must be *optional* —
  see the next point.
- **The paper's own scope warning is a design constraint for a general GPU scheduler**: "in DNN
  training, the tensor shapes are typically static, and thus memory allocation can be optimized
  ahead of time. For another example, in serving DNNs that are not LLMs, an increase in memory
  efficiency may not result in any performance improvement since **the performance is primarily
  compute-bound**. In such scenarios, introducing vLLM's techniques may rather degrade the
  performance due to the extra overhead of memory indirection and non-contiguous block memory."
  For a scheduler covering CNN vision and training as well as LLM serving, paging must be a
  per-workload policy, not a global mechanism.
- **Block size is a joint kernel/scheduler parameter.** 16 is the default because it is "large
  enough to efficiently utilize the GPU and small enough to avoid significant internal
  fragmentation." A block is what one warp reads (vLLM assigns a GPU warp per block). So the
  block size ties the memory manager's granularity to the kernel's *subgroup* width — precisely a
  cooperative-tile concern.
- **Three custom kernels exist purely to fight launch overhead**, and all three are
  scatter/gather shapes: (1) *fused reshape and block write* — split the new KV, reshape to a
  block-read-optimized layout, and write to block-table positions, "fused into a single kernel to
  minimize kernel launch overheads"; (2) *fused block read and attention*, one warp per block,
  with variable sequence lengths inside a batch; (3) *fused block copy* — copy-on-write produces
  "numerous invocations of small data movements"; a single kernel batches them instead of many
  `cudaMemcpyAsync` calls. Generalization: **the scheduler surface needs a batched-small-copy
  primitive**, or it will pay N launches for N block copies.
- **Preemption is gang-scheduled and all-or-nothing.** "Since we know that all blocks of a
  sequence are accessed together, we implement an all-or-nothing eviction policy… multiple
  sequences within one request (e.g., beam candidates in one beam search request) are
  gang-scheduled as a *sequence group*." A scheduler that can preempt must know which buffers form
  an indivisible residency unit.
- **Recomputation vs swapping is a policy the scheduler chooses per block size**, and the paper
  gives the rule: recomputation when blocks are small, swapping when blocks are large; recompute
  is never worse than 20% of swap. That is the generic "spill vs recompute" decision a
  frame-budget policy will need.

### 2.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per resident pool: total slots, occupancy, block size, per-item block list, block reference
counts, and which items form a gang. Per launch: whether its KV read is contiguous or paged
(so the ~25% attention penalty is accounted), the block table buffer as part of the read set, and
whether any write is copy-on-write (may allocate). Per item: a maximum-length reservation for
admission and the recompute cost, so eviction can compare recompute against swap bandwidth.

### 2.5 Testable requirements

1. When a resident buffer pool is declared, the scheduler must track occupancy and refuse to admit
   a new item whose reservation exceeds free capacity, rather than failing at first touch.
2. When items are declared a gang, preemption must evict all or none of the gang's buffers.
3. When a paged layout is selected for a kernel, the scheduler must attribute the indirection cost
   (measured, ~20–26% for attention) to that kernel only, and must not apply paging to kernels
   whose arithmetic character is compute-bound.
4. When many small copies are required (copy-on-write, block moves), the surface must express them
   as one batched launch, and a test must show one launch, not N.
5. When the block size is changed, the mapping "one subgroup reads one block" must remain valid,
   and a block size below the subgroup width must be rejected.
6. When memory pressure forces eviction, the policy must choose between recompute and spill using
   measured costs; recompute must be selected when block size is small.
7. When a prefix is shared across items, the block reference count must prevent its free while any
   item references it, and a copy-on-write must fire exactly when refcount > 1.

## 3. Agrawal et al. 2024 — Sarathi-Serve: Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference (OSDI'24)

**The single best source in this set for the numeric roofline crossover.** Everything in
§Synthesis about "at what batch/sequence size does decode become compute-bound" comes from here.

### 3.1 Core mechanism

Two ideas. **Chunked-prefills**: split a prompt's prefill into near-equal chunks sized so that
each chunk is still large enough to saturate GPU compute, and compute the prefill over several
iterations. **Stall-free batching**: build every iteration's batch by first packing *all*
running decodes, then any partially-completed prefill chunk, then new requests — all within a
fixed **token budget τ** derived from the TBT (time-between-tokens) SLO. Because decode
iterations are memory-bound with idle compute, prefill chunks are *piggybacked* into that slack
"for free," so a new request never pauses ongoing decodes (no *generation stall*), and every
iteration has near-uniform compute cost (which also removes pipeline bubbles). It builds on
Orca's iteration-level batching but adds the token throttle Orca lacks.

### 3.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

Models/hardware: Mistral-7B on 1×A100; Yi-34B on 2×A100 (TP2); LLaMA2-70B on 8×A40 (TP4-PP2);
Falcon-180B on 4×A100 × 2 nodes (TP4-PP2, 100 Gbps ethernet).

**The crossover (the number to build the spec around):**
- Figure 5 + footnote 2, LLaMA2-70B linear layers on 4×A100: arithmetic intensity crosses the
  device's FLOPS:bandwidth ratio at **~200 tokens theoretically on A100, but ~500–600 tokens in
  practice at higher tensor-parallel degrees, because of fixed overheads.** Below that: memory
  bound, low MFU. Above: compute bound, low MBU.
- Figure 6, LLaMA2-70B linear-layer execution time vs token count: **execution time is
  essentially flat from 128 to 512 tokens** (pure weight-fetch time), then **rises linearly**
  past the threshold. TP-4 is flatter/cheaper than TP-2.
- Figure 4, Mistral-7B on 1×A100: "**the cost of the linear operation for 1 decode token is
  nearly the same as 128 prefill tokens.**"
- Figure 4 again: "**linear operators contribute more than 80% of total time even at high
  sequence lengths**", though attention grows quadratically. At 2K prefill, total ≈145 ms with
  attention a visible but minority slice; decode at any batch 1→64 is ≈15–20 ms and dominated by
  linear layers.
- Figure 3, Mistral-7B, prompt 1024, 1×A100: **prefill throughput saturates at batch size 1**
  (~4–5K tok/s, essentially flat over batch 1/2/4/8); **decode throughput scales near-linearly
  with batch** (≈10 → 100 → 200 → 420 → 800 tok/s at batch 1/8/16/32/64). Note the *different
  y-axes*: prefill is an order of magnitude more efficient per token.

**Cost of mixing prefill into a decode batch** (Figure 9; ratio vs a decode-only batch):

"Naive hybrid batching leads to a dramatic increase of **up to 28.3×** in TBT latency compared
to a decode-only batch." Chunking bounds it to **1.2–3.7×**.

**Other hard numbers:**
- **Tile quantization: chunk size 257 increases prefill time by 32% versus chunk size 256.**
  A one-token misalignment costs a third of the kernel. This is the most directly actionable
  compiler/scheduler fact in the paper.
- Chunking overhead in prefill compute (Yi-34B TP-2, prompt 2K/4K/8K): **≤ ~25% at chunk 512**,
  **≈ negligible at chunk 2048**. Cause: chunk *i* must re-read the KV of all prior chunks, so
  an `N`-chunk prefill re-loads chunk 1's KV `N−1` times — yet "even at small chunk sizes the
  attention prefill operation is compute bound," so the extra cost is fixed launch/geometry
  overhead more than bandwidth.
- Token budgets actually used: **τ = 512 under strict SLO, τ = 2048 under relaxed**, and
  **τ = 1536 for LLaMA2-70B relaxed** specifically to reduce pipeline bubbles.
- Pipeline bubble magnitude, Falcon-180B: one 4K-token prompt takes **≈1150 ms** while a
  decode-only iteration at batch 32 takes **≈200 ms** → interleaving them creates a **≈950 ms
  bubble**.
- SLO definitions used: strict P99 TBT = 5× and relaxed = 25× the execution time of a decode
  iteration (4K prefill, batch 32). Absolute: Mistral-7B 0.1 s / 0.5 s; Yi-34B 0.2 s / 1 s;
  LLaMA2-70B and Falcon-180B 1 s / 5 s.
- Capacity gains: Mistral-7B **2.6×**, Yi-34B **3.7×**, LLaMA2-70B **6.3×** vs Orca / **4.3×** vs
  vLLM, Falcon-180B **5.6×** end-to-end with PP. Mistral-7B with τ=512 gets **3.5× capacity at a
  100 ms strict SLO**; with τ=2048, 1.65× at a 1 s SLO (Yi-34B).
- Ablation (Table 4, Yi-34B, τ=1024): hybrid-batching-only P50 TTFT 0.53 s / P99 TBT 0.68 s;
  chunked-prefills-only 1.04 / 0.17; **combined 0.76 / 0.14**. Each technique alone regresses one
  metric; only together do both improve.
- Workload shape (Table 2): `openchat_sharegpt4` prompt median 1730 / P90 5696 tokens, output
  median 415; `arxiv_summarization` prompt median **7059** / P90 12985, output median 208.
  **Prompt:output ≈ 4:1 to 34:1** — prefill is not a rounding error.

### 3.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications (single process, one GPU)

- **The phase model is a roofline model, and the axis is `tokens in the batch`, not `requests`.**
  For every *linear/GEMM* kernel in a transformer, the input is a `[T, d] × [d, k]` GEMM where
  `T` = total tokens in the iteration. `T < ~200` (theory) / `~500–600` (practice) ⇒
  memory-bound (weight-fetch-limited, time ≈ constant in `T`); above ⇒ compute-bound (time
  linear in `T`). Prefill of a 1730-token prompt is one `T = 1730` GEMM: compute-bound. Decode at
  batch 32 is one `T = 32` GEMM: memory-bound, and **costs the same wall time as `T = 128`**.
- **Therefore the free-lunch rule**: any decode iteration has `budget = T_crossover − T_decode`
  tokens of *free* compute. Filling it with prefill chunk tokens raises throughput at ~zero
  latency cost. This is the entire Sarathi mechanism, and it is a *kernel-level* observation that
  happens to be exploited by a request scheduler. In a single-process cajeta scheduler the same
  observation licenses **co-running a compute-bound kernel with a memory-bound one**, which is
  exactly the "complementary kernels" premise.
- **Attention is the exception and must be classified separately from the linear layers.**
  Prefill attention is compute-bound and grows as `O(T²)`; decode attention is memory-bound and
  grows as `O(batch × context)` in bytes. The linear layers are >80% of time, but the attention
  kernel is the one whose cost depends on KV-cache residency. A per-layer tag is wrong; the tag
  must be **per-kernel** and must be derived from that kernel's own shape.
- **A mixed (hybrid) batch produces a ragged attention launch.** In one iteration, request A
  contributes 1 query token against 4096 KV, and request B contributes 512 query tokens against
  512 KV. A single attention kernel over that batch has wildly different per-item cost — this is
  precisely the imbalance FlashInfer's cost model `α·l_q + β·l_kv` exists to schedule. Sarathi's
  implementation notes it uses FlashAttention-v2 and FlashInfer paged-chunk-prefill kernels for
  exactly this.
- **Tile quantization is a first-class scheduler constraint.** 257 vs 256 tokens = +32% prefill
  time. Any chunking policy must round the chunk size to the kernel's tile granularity, and the
  submit seam must expose that granularity so the policy can. This generalizes: *the scheduler
  must never choose a geometry the kernel cannot tile evenly.*
- **Uniform-cost iterations are what remove bubbles.** For pipeline parallelism the mechanism is
  micro-batch balance; inside one process, the same property makes co-scheduling predictable —
  if every submitted kernel's cost is within a narrow band, a frame-budget policy can actually
  hold a budget.
- Launch-overhead sensitivity: the paper attributes the theory-vs-practice gap (200 → 500–600
  tokens) to "fixed overheads," and attributes small-chunk overhead to "fixed overheads of kernel
  launch, etc." **At the memory-bound end of the curve, launch overhead is a first-order term.**

### 3.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per iteration: total token count `T` (this is the roofline coordinate); per-request
`(query_tokens, kv_context_len)` pairs; the kernel's tile granularity for each dimension (so
chunk sizes round correctly); a token budget τ derived from a latency target; whether a request's
prefill is partial (and which chunk index, so the KV re-read cost is predictable); and per-kernel
`(FLOPs, bytes)` so the crossover point can be computed rather than hard-coded per device.

### 3.5 Testable requirements

1. When a GEMM-shaped kernel is submitted with total token count `T` below the target's measured
   crossover, the scheduler must tag it memory-bound; when above, compute-bound. The crossover
   must be a *measured per-device* constant, not a literal.
2. When the scheduler forms a co-run group, it must not place two compute-bound kernels together
   if a memory-bound kernel is runnable; a memory-bound + compute-bound pair must be preferred.
3. When a chunk size is chosen, it must be a multiple of the kernel's declared tile granularity;
   a non-multiple must be rejected or rounded (257 vs 256 costs 32%).
4. When a latency policy declares a per-iteration budget, the sum of submitted kernels' estimated
   costs must not exceed it, and a kernel that would exceed it must be split (chunked) rather
   than dropped or run.
5. When a batch mixes items with different query lengths and KV lengths, the attention launch
   must carry per-item `(l_q, l_kv)` and must be balanced by cost, not by item count.
6. When a prefill is split into `N` chunks, the scheduler must account for the KV re-read of all
   prior chunks in the cost estimate for chunk `i` (it is `Σ_{j<i} len_j` extra bytes).
7. When decode-batch occupancy leaves free compute (measured `T < T_crossover`), the scheduler
   must be able to admit additional work in the same iteration without a new device round-trip.
8. When measured, admitting a full (unchunked) prefill alongside decodes must be observable as a
   >5× latency spike on the decode stream — the regression test for "the throttle is actually
   engaged."

## 4. Patel et al. 2024 — Splitwise: Efficient Generative LLM Inference Using Phase Splitting (ISCA'24)

Mostly a cluster-placement paper, but its **characterization section is the best measured
evidence that prefill and decode are different roofline regimes**, and it is measured on
production Azure traces, not synthetic ones.

### 4.1 Core mechanism

Characterize prompt (prefill) and token-generation (decode) phases separately on A100 and H100,
then run them on *separate machines*: a prompt pool, a token pool, and a mixed pool that expands
and contracts. When a request arrives, the cluster-level scheduler assigns a (prompt machine,
token machine) pair by join-the-shortest-queue; the prompt machine computes the prefill and
streams the KV cache to the token machine over InfiniBand, **layer by layer, overlapped with the
prompt computation of the next layer** (an async transfer is triggered at the end of each
layer). Machine-level schedulers do FCFS with a prompt-batch cap and, on mixed machines,
preempt token work for prompts with age-based anti-starvation.

### 4.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- **The memory wall, tabulated** (Table I, DGX-A100 vs DGX-H100): TFLOPs 19.5 → 66.9
  (**3.43×**); HBM capacity 80 GB → 80 GB (**1.00×**); HBM bandwidth 2039 → 3352 GB/s
  (**1.64×**); power 400 → 700 W (1.75×); NVLink 50 → 100 Gbps (2×); InfiniBand 200 → 400 Gbps
  (2×); cost/machine $17.6 → $38/hr (2.16×). *Compute is outrunning bandwidth by ~2× per
  generation and capacity is flat.* Every memory-bound kernel gets relatively worse on each new
  GPU.
- Models: Llama2-70B (80 layers, hidden 8192, 32 heads) and BLOOM-176B (70 layers, 14336, 112
  heads) on **8×H100**, vLLM.
- Production traces (Azure, 11 Nov 2023): **coding** — median prompt **1500** tokens, median
  output **13** tokens; **conversation** — median prompt **1020**, median output **129**
  (bimodal). Prompt:output ratios of ~115:1 and ~8:1.
- **Decode is memory-bound, quantified**: "With a batch size of 64, there is only **2× impact on
  TBT**" — 64× the tokens for 2× the time. And Fig. 6b: token-phase throughput "keeps increasing
  with batching until 64 batch-size, at which point the machine runs out of memory." Decode is
  limited by **memory capacity**, not compute.
- **Prefill is compute-bound and saturates early**: TTFT grows *almost linearly* with prompt size;
  prompt-phase throughput **decreases after 2048 prompt tokens**, i.e. a batch size of less than
  2 for median prompts. Insight IV: "The prompt phase batch size should be limited… In contrast,
  batching the token generation phase yields high throughput without any downside."
- **Batch occupancy in practice is tiny**: "most of the time (**60–70%**) for conversation is
  spent running only **20 tokens or fewer**. Since the coding service has very few output tokens,
  it experiences even worse batching in the token phase and runs with a **single token for more
  than 20% of the time**." The real decode workload lives deep in the launch-bound regime.
- **Phase cost ratio**: "for BLOOM-176B, a prompt phase with 1500 input tokens takes the same time
  as a token phase with only **6 output tokens**." So one prefill token ≈ 1/250 of a decode step
  in cost.
- **Hardware sensitivity separates the phases** (Table IV, P50, Llama-70B, no batching, A100→H100):
  TTFT 185→95 ms (**0.51×**) but TBT 52→31 ms (**0.70×**) on coding; 155→84 (0.54×) and 40→28
  (0.70×) on conversation. **A 3.43× compute upgrade buys prefill ~2× and decode only ~1.4×** —
  decode tracks the 1.64× bandwidth ratio, not the compute ratio. Energy per request was *equal
  or worse* on H100 for decode (1.37 vs 1.37 Whr coding; 7.9 vs 9.4 Whr conversation).
- **Power** (Insight VI): prompt-phase power rises with batch size toward TDP and prompt latency
  is highly sensitive to power caps; **token generation shows almost no latency impact when the
  GPU is power-capped by more than 50% (700 W → 350 W)**, and its draw barely varies with token
  count.
- Memory: during prompt phase the footprint stays near the model size (~300 GB for BLOOM-176B);
  during token phase it climbs past 500 GB as batched tokens grow (Fig. 7).
- KV transfer overlap: serialized transfer latency grows linearly with prompt size (to ~40 ms at
  2048 batched tokens on A100) and reaches 3% of E2E; **layer-wise overlapped transfer leaves a
  constant non-overlapped residue of ~8 ms (A100) / ~5 ms (H100)** and costs 0.8% of E2E.
  Splitwise picks serialized for small prompts (<512 tokens on H100) and per-layer above that.
- End result: 1.4× throughput at 20% lower cost; 2.35× throughput at the same power and cost;
  1.76× throughput at 15% lower power and equal cost.

### 4.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications (single process, one GPU)

- **The two phases have *opposite* limiting resources, and this is now measured three independent
  ways** — time vs batch (2× for 64×), hardware sensitivity (0.70× vs 0.51× on an A100→H100
  upgrade), and power sensitivity (no latency change at half power). For a single-process
  scheduler the immediate consequence is that a prefill kernel and a decode kernel are the
  canonical **complementary pair**: one saturates the matrix cores and the power budget, the
  other saturates HBM and leaves both idle. This is the strongest paper-backed justification for
  the cajeta co-run premise.
- **Power/clock is a shared resource the scheduler should model.** A compute-bound kernel running
  alone pushes the GPU to TDP and can cause clock throttling; a memory-bound kernel does not.
  Co-running them means the *pair* must fit the power budget — and the paper's data says the
  memory-bound half is nearly free power-wise. On an APU like gfx1151, where CPU and GPU share
  one power budget, this is not a theoretical concern.
- **Prefill batching has a hard cap and decode batching does not.** "Throughput decreases after
  2048 prompt tokens." So a scheduler's admission rule is asymmetric: cap the compute-bound
  kernel's work per launch at the point where it saturates, and grow the memory-bound kernel's
  batch until residency runs out.
- **The layer-wise overlap pattern is a general dependency-granularity lesson.** Splitwise turned
  a serialized whole-model dependency (prefill → transfer → decode) into 80 per-layer
  dependencies, converting a 40 ms serial cost into an 8 ms residue. Inside one process the
  analogue is that a submit seam should express dependencies at the **buffer-region** level (this
  layer's KV slice) rather than the whole-buffer level, so a consumer can start on layer 0's
  output while layer 1 is still producing.
- **Small-transfer policy switch**: below 512 tokens Splitwise uses the *simple serialized*
  transfer because the fine-grained path's per-layer synchronization costs more than it saves.
  Generalizes to: fine-grained scheduling has a fixed cost, and below some work size the coarse
  path wins. Any cajeta policy that splits work must have this cut-off and must be able to
  measure it.
- Its own scope note is relevant: Splitwise says its approach "is applicable to any hardware
  (including CPUs, FPGAs, ASICs) that aligns with the computational requirements of prompt and
  token phases" and calls out **AMD MI-250** and HBM-equipped CPUs as candidate token machines.

### 4.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per launch: phase tag; expected power class (does this kernel push toward TDP?); prompt/prefill
token count with a saturation cap; decode batch size with a residency cap; and per-layer output
region descriptors so a consumer can be released incrementally. Per device: measured
FLOPS:bandwidth ratio and the TDP headroom, so the compute-bound/memory-bound classification and
the co-run power check are both computable.

### 4.5 Testable requirements

1. When a compute-bound kernel and a memory-bound kernel are both runnable, the scheduler must
   prefer to co-run them over co-running two of the same class.
2. When a launch is tagged compute-bound, the scheduler must cap its per-launch work at the
   measured saturation point (for prefill, ~2048 tokens on the measured device) rather than
   growing it with available work.
3. When a launch is tagged memory-bound, the scheduler must grow its batch until the residency
   limit, not until a compute limit.
4. When two kernels are co-scheduled, the sum of their power classes must be checked against the
   device budget, and a compute+compute pair that would exceed it must be serialized.
5. When a producer writes a buffer in per-layer (or per-region) slices, the seam must allow a
   consumer to be released per slice rather than on whole-buffer completion.
6. When the work in a launch is below the fine-grained path's measured break-even, the scheduler
   must select the coarse path.
7. When the same model is measured on two devices with different FLOPS:bandwidth ratios, the
   phase classification must change accordingly with no source change.

## 5. Zhong et al. 2024 — DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized LLM Serving (OSDI'24)

### 5.1 Core mechanism

Assign prefill and decode to *different* GPUs, eliminating prefill–decode interference entirely,
and then independently choose each phase's parallelism (intra-op / tensor vs inter-op / pipeline)
and replica count to maximize **per-GPU goodput** — the request rate sustainable while meeting
both a TTFT SLO and a TPOT SLO for ≥90% of requests. A placement algorithm searches the
parallelism × replica space with a simulator; the runtime transfers the KV cache from prefill to
decode instances, colocating same-stage segments so the transfer uses NVLink. Scheduling is FCFS
with two refinements: prefill batches are built to a total sequence length near the measured
saturation point `L_m`, and decode instances **pull** KV from prefill instances as needed rather
than being pushed, using the prefill GPU's memory as a queue buffer.

### 5.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Testbed: 4 nodes × 8 **NVIDIA A100-80GB SXM** with NVLINK, 25 Gbps cross-node.
- **The interference measurement** (13B model, Figure 2): a batch of decodes alone vs the same
  batch with **one** prefill job added — at input length 128, ≈5–10 ms → ≈15–25 ms; at input
  length **1024, ≈20–50 ms → ≈120–140 ms**. *One prefill request inflates a decode iteration by
  3–6×.* This is the single-GPU number that motivates everything else.
- **Saturation point**: "for a 13B LLM, computing the prefill of a **512-token sequence** makes an
  A100 **near compute-bound**." Named `L_m`, "a critical input length threshold beyond which the
  prefill phase becomes compute-bound. Batching more requests should only be considered when the
  input length of the scheduled request is below `L_m`."
- Figure 3, 13B: **prefill throughput is flat in batch size for input ≥ 512** (already saturated
  at batch 1–2; ~7000 tok/s at 512, ~5500 at 1024), while short inputs (128) still gain up to
  batch ~16 (~9000 tok/s). **Decode throughput rises roughly linearly with batch to 128**
  (~200 → ~2500 tok/s), nearly independent of input length.
- **Decode eventually becomes compute-bound**: "As the decoding batch size continues to increase,
  the decoding computation begins to resemble the prefill phase." Paged-attention/GQA "enable
  further scaling of the decoding batch size to nearly compute-bound."
- Goodput at 90% SLO attainment, 13B, 512-in/64-out, one A100-80GB: **colocated 1.6 rps;
  prefill-only 5.6 rps; decode-only 10 rps.** Allocating 2 prefill GPUs + 1 decode GPU gives
  10 rps overall = 3.3 rps/GPU = **2.1× the colocated system**.
- Parallelism for decode (Figure 5, 13B, batch 128, input 256): intra-op (tensor parallel) latency
  62 → 40 → 35 → 32 ms for 1/2/4/8 GPUs (**strongly diminishing**), throughput sublinear;
  inter-op (pipeline) latency flat at ~62 ms but throughput **scales nearly linearly**. Rule
  given: "when the TPOT SLO is stringent, intra-op parallelism is essential… Beyond this,
  inter-op parallelism is preferable to enhance throughput."
- KV transfer: a single 512-token request on OPT-66B has a **1.13 GB** KV cache; at 10 rps that
  is 11.3 GB/s ≈ 90 Gbps. Measured: KV transfer is **<0.1% of total latency for OPT-175B**, and
  **>95% of requests see <30 ms** transfer delay, because same-stage colocation keeps it on
  NVLink (600 GB/s peak A100–A100).
- End result: **7.4× more requests or 12.6× tighter SLO**; 2.0–4.6× higher rate than vLLM on
  ShareGPT, 1.6–7.4× vs DeepSpeed-MII. Simulator error <2%.
- Workloads (Table 1 / Figure 7): ShareGPT input avg **755.5**, output avg **200.3**; HumanEval
  input avg **1713**, output **98.2**; LongBench input avg **1738.3**, output **90.7**. SLOs:
  chatbot OPT-13B TTFT 0.25 s / TPOT 0.1 s; code completion OPT-66B 0.125 s / 0.2 s;
  summarization OPT-66B 15 s / 0.15 s.
- Implementation: 6.5K lines Python + **8.1K lines C++/CUDA**; "asynchronous CudaMemcpy for
  intra-node communication, which avoids blocking the GPU computation during transmission."

### 5.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **This paper is the strongest published argument *against* naive co-running.** Putting a
  compute-bound kernel next to a memory-bound one in the *same* launch (a hybrid batch) does not
  give you the memory-bound kernel's latency; it gives you something close to the compute-bound
  one's. The 3–6× decode slowdown from one added prefill is the measurement. For cajeta this
  means: **co-running must be resource-partitioned (separate CU/SM sets, separate queues), not
  merely "put both in one batch."** FlashInfer's user-supplied SM count and Nanoflow's fixed
  SM-per-stream partition are the mechanism DistServe's numbers demand.
- **The `L_m` concept is exactly the metadata a submit seam should carry.** DistServe profiles
  each (model, GPU) pair for the shortest total token count that saturates the device, then
  schedules prefill batches at total length ≈ `L_m` — batching several short requests together or
  running a long one alone. That is a per-kernel, per-device measured constant, and it is the same
  quantity Sarathi calls the token budget crossover (~500–600 tokens for LLaMA2-70B on 4×A100,
  ~512 for a 13B on 1×A100).
- **"The number of new tokens in the batch is a reliable indicator of the batch's real execution
  time."** A one-scalar cost model. Any cajeta cost estimator for transformer launches can start
  here and refine.
- **The chunked-prefill critique is a real cost the cajeta spec must acknowledge.** Splitting a
  prefill into `N` chunks forces chunk `i` to re-read the KV of chunks `1..i-1`, so total KV
  loaded is `N + (N−1) + … + 1 = O(N²)` chunk-loads versus `O(N)` unchunked. Also: too-small
  chunks fail to saturate; too-large chunks leave no room to piggyback decodes. So **chunk size
  has a two-sided constraint** — at least `L_m`-ish to saturate, at most `τ − decode_tokens` to
  hold the latency budget — and the `O(N²)` term grows with context length. Sarathi measures the
  cost at ≤25% (chunk 512) and ~0 (chunk 2048); DistServe explains why it is not zero.
- **Parallelism choice depends on the latency target, not just the shape.** Intra-op (splitting
  one kernel across more CUs) reduces latency with diminishing returns and lost utilization;
  inter-op (pipelining independent kernels) preserves throughput scaling but not latency. A cajeta
  `schedule policy` of *latency* vs *throughput* maps directly onto this: latency ⇒ split the
  kernel wider; throughput ⇒ run more kernels concurrently at their efficient widths.
- **Transfers should be non-blocking and stage-aligned.** DistServe's async copy "avoids blocking
  the GPU computation during transmission," and same-stage colocation is what keeps the transfer
  off the slow path. In-process, the analogue is that a copy/DMA engine's work must be schedulable
  concurrently with compute, with the dependency expressed at stage granularity.
- **A "pull" (consumer-initiated) handoff prevents memory blowup under burst.** When the producer
  outruns the consumer, buffering in the producer's memory and letting the consumer fetch on
  demand is the back-pressure mechanism. A submit seam with a bounded output queue needs the same.

### 5.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per device+kernel: the measured saturation token count `L_m`. Per launch: total new-token count
(the cost proxy), phase, and the parallelism width actually granted. Per policy: TTFT-analogue and
TPOT-analogue budgets (a first-result deadline and a steady-state per-step deadline are *different*
budgets and both must be expressible). Per buffer: whether a transfer is required and whether it
can be split by stage.

### 5.5 Testable requirements

1. When a compute-bound and a memory-bound kernel are co-scheduled, they must be granted disjoint
   compute-unit sets; a test must show the memory-bound kernel's latency rises by less than a
   configured bound (contrast: a shared-batch baseline shows 3–6×).
2. When the scheduler builds a compute-bound launch, it must target a total work size within a
   configured band of the measured `L_m` for that (kernel, device), batching several small items or
   running one large item alone.
3. When `L_m` has not been measured for a (kernel, device) pair, the scheduler must measure it
   (a profiling pass) rather than assume a default.
4. When a prefill is chunked into `N` chunks, the cost model must include the `O(N²)` KV re-read
   term and the scheduler must reject chunk counts where that term exceeds a configured fraction
   of the launch cost.
5. When the schedule policy is `latency`, the scheduler must widen a single kernel across compute
   units; when it is `throughput`, it must prefer more concurrent kernels at their efficient
   widths. Both must be observable in the emitted grid geometry.
6. When a policy declares both a first-result deadline and a per-step deadline, violating either
   must be reported separately.
7. When a producer's output queue is full, the producer must block (or spill to its own buffer)
   rather than allocate unboundedly.
8. When a copy is submitted, it must be schedulable concurrently with compute on the same device
   and must not force a device-wide barrier.

## 6. Leviathan, Kalman & Matias 2023 — Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding (ICML'23)

### 6.1 Core mechanism

Run a small **approximation model** `M_q` autoregressively for `γ` steps to produce `γ` guessed
tokens, then run the large **target model** `M_p` **once, in parallel, over all `γ+1` positions**
to get `p₁…p_{γ+1}`. Accept guess `i` with probability `min(1, p_i(x)/q_i(x))`; on the first
rejection, resample that position from the adjusted distribution `norm(max(0, p−q))` and discard
the rest. This **speculative sampling** rule is proved to leave the output distribution *exactly*
equal to sampling from `M_p` — no retraining, no architecture change, no output change. The
target model always produces at least one token, so the worst case is never worse than standard
decoding.

### 6.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Hardware: a **single TPU-v4**, batch size 1, T5X codebase.
- Expected tokens per iteration: `E = (1 − α^{γ+1})/(1 − α)` where `α = E(β)` is the acceptance
  rate. Walltime improvement: `(1 − α^{γ+1}) / ((1 − α)(γc + 1))`, `c` = cost ratio of one `M_q`
  run to one `M_p` run.
- **Measured on T5-XXL (11B) target**: EnDe translation, T5-small (77M) draft, temp=0, γ=7,
  α=0.75 → **3.4×**; temp=1, γ=7, α=0.62 → **2.6×**. CNN/DM summarization, T5-small, temp=0,
  γ=5, α=0.65 → **3.1×**; temp=1, γ=5, α=0.53 → **2.3×**. Independent reproduction on
  **Chinchilla 70B: 2–2.5×**.
- **The compute/speed trade-off table** (Table 1, `c = ĉ = 0`):

  | α | γ | total ops | speed |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 0.6 | 2 | 1.53× | 1.96× |
  | 0.7 | 3 | 1.58× | 2.53× |
  | 0.8 | 2 | 1.23× | 2.44× |
  | 0.8 | 5 | 1.63× | 3.69× |
  | 0.9 | 2 | 1.11× | 2.71× |
  | 0.9 | 10 | **1.60×** | **6.86×** |

  **1.1–1.6× more arithmetic buys 2–6.9× less wall time.** The extra FLOPs are nearly free
  because they land in idle compute.
- **The load-bearing sentence for a kernel scheduler**: "Unlike the total number of arithmetic
  operations, the **total number of memory accesses can go down** with our method. Specifically,
  the target model's weights and KV cache can be read **once per execution of Algorithm 1**, so
  the number of memory accesses for reading them shrinks by a factor of `(1 − α^{γ+1})/(1 − α)`."
  Speculative decoding is *literally* a transformation from memory-bound to compute-bound.
- Acceptance rates `α` measured (Table 3): GPT-like 97M target with a 6M draft → **0.88–0.89**;
  with a bigram model → 0.05; unigram → 0.03. T5-XXL EnDe: T5-small 0.62–0.75, T5-base 0.68–0.80,
  T5-large 0.71–0.82; bigram 0.19–0.20. LaMDA 137B: 100M draft 0.57–0.61, 2B 0.71, 8B 0.74–0.75.
  Guidance: "choosing `M_q` to be around **two orders of magnitude smaller** than `M_p` usually
  performed best."
- Optimal `γ` (Figure 3): with `c = 0.05`, α = 0.7 → γ ≈ 4; α = 0.9 → γ ≈ 10–12. With `c = 0.01`,
  γ climbs to ~20+ at α = 0.9. **`γ` is a tuned function of `α` and `c`, not a constant.**
- Zero-cost drafts (n-gram table lookup, `c ≈ 0`): improvement → `1/(1−α)`; e.g. T5-XXL with a
  bigram draft (α ≈ 0.2, γ = 3) still gives **1.25×**.
- Lenience (relaxing exactness) at `c = 0.015`, T5-XXL/T5-small EnDe: l = 1/0.5/0.3/0.1 give
  α = 0.62/0.71/0.76/0.84 and improvement 2.5×/3.1×/3.6×/**5×**.
- The scope condition, stated by the authors: "our method is **not helpful for configurations
  where additional computation resources are not available**. However, in common cases where
  additional computation resources are available (e.g. when memory bandwidth is the bottleneck)
  our method provides the speedup with significant benefits."

### 6.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **Two kernel streams that must alternate but have opposite characters.** Per iteration: `γ`
  small, serial, deeply launch-bound `M_q` decode steps, then **one** `M_p` forward over `γ+1`
  positions — which is a *tiny prefill*, i.e. a compute-heavier, higher-intensity kernel than an
  ordinary decode step. Between them the dependency is strict: the draft's `i`-th token feeds the
  draft's `i+1`-th step, and all `γ` must exist before the verify. So *within one request there is
  no draft/verify overlap*. The overlap only exists **across requests**: request A's verify
  (compute-bound) can co-run with request B's draft chain (memory- and launch-bound). That is a
  textbook complementary pair for a single-process scheduler.
- **The draft chain is the launch-overhead worst case in the whole workload class.** `γ = 5–10`
  serial forward passes of a model two orders of magnitude smaller than the target, at batch 1.
  A 6M-parameter draft step moves ~12 MB of weights — on a device with 200+ GB/s that is tens of
  microseconds, comparable to a launch. If the draft model has, say, 12 layers × 6 kernels, that
  is 72 launches per draft token and ~500 launches per accepted-token group. **Speculative
  decoding is only profitable if the per-launch overhead is small; a graph/persistent-kernel path
  for the draft chain is mandatory, not an optimization.**
- **The verify kernel's shape is `γ+1` query tokens against the full KV.** In FlashInfer's terms
  `l_qo = γ+1` (times GQA group size `g`), so its operational intensity is `(γ+1)·g` — for γ=7
  and g=8 that is an effective query length of 64, deep into tensor-core tile territory, whereas a
  plain decode step (`l_qo = 1`, g = 8 → 8) is not. **The same attention operator changes
  arithmetic class purely because of `γ`.** A submit seam that carries `l_qo` gets this for free;
  one that carries only "decode" gets it wrong.
- **Rejection makes the amount of committed work data-dependent.** The number of accepted tokens
  `n` is known only after the verify. Anything downstream (KV commit, cache pointer advance, the
  next draft's starting state) is a *dynamic* amount of work. A scheduler that must pre-declare
  geometry needs either an indirect launch bound or a worst-case launch with a predicate.
- **Tree/beam variants raise the branching factor.** Appendix A.4 sketches beam-search speculation:
  draft with beam `u ≥ w` for `γ` steps, then verify all candidates in parallel at a cost of
  `(w + uγ)` runs of `M_p`. That is a much wider but still single verify launch — and it is what
  later systems call *tree attention* (a custom mask). The mask is data-dependent per step.
- **The whole method is an instance of a general scheduler policy**: spend idle compute to reduce
  memory traffic. Any cajeta policy that classifies a launch as memory-bound has, by the same
  logic, licence to run redundant/speculative compute in its shadow.

### 6.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per iteration: `γ` (the speculation depth — a tunable that depends on measured `α` and `c`), the
draft model's per-step cost `c`, the measured acceptance rate `α` (so `γ` can be adapted online),
the verify launch's query length `γ+1` and its mask shape (linear for plain speculation, tree for
beam), and an *indirect* count of accepted tokens for the commit step. Also: an identity for the
draft-chain launch sequence so it can be captured once and replayed.

### 6.5 Testable requirements

1. When a verify launch is submitted with `γ+1` query tokens, its arithmetic-character tag must be
   computed from `(γ+1)·g` and must differ from a `γ = 0` decode launch of the same operator.
2. When a draft chain of `γ` serial steps is submitted, the scheduler must issue it as a captured
   / replayed sequence, and a test must show the number of host-side submissions per token group is
   `O(1)` rather than `O(γ · layers · kernels)`.
3. When a verify launch for request A is compute-bound and a draft chain for request B is
   memory-bound, the scheduler must be permitted to co-run them.
4. When the number of accepted tokens is only known on-device, the dependent commit launch must
   take its bound from a device buffer (indirect dispatch), not from a host constant.
5. When the measured acceptance rate `α` changes, `γ` must be re-derived from `α` and the measured
   draft/target cost ratio `c`, not left fixed.
6. When a tree/beam speculation mask is used, the mask must be passed as a data-dependent block
   pattern and the scheduler must balance on its nonzero count.
7. When no idle compute is available (the device is already compute-saturated), a speculation
   policy must be able to report that it is not profitable and fall back.

## 7. Ye et al. 2025 — FlashInfer: Efficient and Customizable Attention Engine for LLM Inference Serving (MLSys'25)

**The single most directly applicable paper in this set for the cajeta submit seam.** It is
literally a load-balancing kernel scheduler with a compile-time/run-time split, sitting
inside one process on one GPU.

### 7.1 Core mechanism

FlashInfer unifies every KV-cache layout (paged attention, radix tree, sliding window,
arbitrary sparse mask) into one **block-sparse row (BSR)** matrix with *arbitrary* block
size `(B_r, B_c)` — `B_r` is the query tile size, `B_c` the KV page size — so a single
kernel family covers prefill, decode, shared-prefix, and pruned-KV. A JIT compiler turns
an *attention variant specification* (QueryTransform / KeyTransform / ValueTransform /
OutputTransform / LogitsTransform / LogitsMask functors) into a specialized CUDA/CUTLASS
kernel, so RoPE, soft-cap, sliding window, FlashSigmoid, MLA all reuse one template rather
than one hand-written kernel each. At runtime a **CPU-side `plan()` step** consumes the
current per-request query/KV lengths and emits a work queue mapping (query-tile, KV-chunk)
work items onto CTAs plus a reduction map from partial outputs to final outputs; `run()`
executes it. The plan is computed once per generation step and **reused across all layers**,
amortizing its cost. Attention and the partial-output contraction are merged into one
persistent kernel. Grid size is fixed at compile time and workspace offsets are stable, so
the whole thing stays CUDAGraph-capturable despite the dynamism.

### 7.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Hardware: NVIDIA **H100 80GB SXM** and **A100 40GB SXM**, CUDA 12.4, PyTorch 2.4.0, fp16.
- **29–69% inter-token-latency (ITL) reduction** vs a Triton backend on the LLM serving
  benchmark; **28–30%** latency reduction for long-context (StreamingLLM); **13–17%**
  speedup for parallel generation.
- **Load-balancing ablation is the money table** (Llama-3.1-8B, H100 SXM5, SGLang):
  ITL ms — `U(4096,16384)` at request rate 1: **8.63 with load balancing vs 13.89 without**
  (Triton 11.08) → **38% of ITL was pure scheduling loss**. `U(512,2048)` RR=8: 8.21 vs 8.42.
  ShareGPT RR=16: 8.96 vs 9.16. TTFT ms, `U(4096,16384)` RR=1: 411.02 vs 421.60 (Triton 566.30).
  *Skew in sequence lengths is what makes the scheduler matter; uniform short workloads
  barely care.*
- Fine-grained block sparsity, batch-decode, block 16, 32 q-heads / 32 kv-heads, d=128, H100:
  FlashInfer **20.3–68.7 µs** vs PyTorch SDPA **287–1712 µs** vs FlexAttention **1070–1187 µs**
  → up to **20×**, because SDPA/FlexAttention require large block templates.
- Shared-prefix composable format, suffix 128: prefix 32768 @ BS=16 → **217.42 µs composable
  vs 945.67 µs single-format** (4.3×); prefix 1024 @ BS=16 → 45.17 vs 46.52 (no gain).
  *Composability pays only when the shared prefix is large.*
- Fused RoPE+attention kernel: **1.6–3.7× higher bandwidth utilization** than RoPE and
  attention as separate kernels.
- vLLM integration, throughput/ITL/TTFT: bf16 default 6062.89 tok/s / 10.42 / 35.85 vs
  FlashInfer 6065.41 / 10.63 / 36.60; **fp8 e4m3 default 6015.86 / 12.56 / 39.74 vs
  FlashInfer 6020.32 / 10.92 / 37.93** (≈13% ITL cut with an fp8 KV-cache).
- Sparse-vs-dense KV gathering overhead: **<1% for decode kernels, ≈10% for prefill kernels**
  (prefill on FA3/Hopper cannot use TMA for sparse gather, needs smaller KV tiles, spills).

### 7.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications (single process, one GPU)

**The roofline formula is stated explicitly and is the cleanest classifier in the whole set.**
FlashAttention's operational intensity is

    I = O( 1 / (1/l_qo + 1/l_kv) )

where `l_qo` is query length and `l_kv` is KV-cache length. In LLM serving `l_qo` is either
== `l_kv` (prefill) or ≪ `l_kv` (decode), so **I simplifies to `O(l_qo)`** — the arithmetic
character of an attention kernel is determined by *query length alone*, not batch size.
Two consequences the paper states outright:

1. **Batching (Orca-style continuous batching) does NOT change the operational intensity of
   attention.** Adding more decode requests to a batch adds work and adds KV traffic in the
   same proportion. Decode attention stays memory-bound at *every* batch size. (Contrast with
   the GEMM/FFN kernels, which *do* become compute-bound with batch — see the Synthesis.)
2. **GQA raises it**: with group size `g = H_qo/H_kv`, intensity becomes `O(g · l_qo)`.
   Head-group fusion maps different KV heads to different threadblocks and *fuses the query
   heads into the query-length dimension*, giving an effective query length `l'_qo = g·l_qo`.
   So a GQA-8 decode kernel behaves like an 8-token prefill tile, not like a 1-token one.

FlashInfer confirms this by measuring **bandwidth utilization for decode kernels and FLOPs
utilization for prefill kernels** (Figure 8) — i.e. the paper itself uses different roofline
metrics per phase.

Other scheduling-relevant findings:

- **Tile size is a scheduling parameter, not a kernel constant.** FA2 kernels are instantiated
  at query tile sizes `(1, 16, 32, 64, 128) × KV tiles (32, 64, 128)`. Query tile 1 must use
  **CUDA cores** (tensor-core `mma` has a 16-row minimum); ≥16 uses tensor cores; FA3/Hopper
  needs row tiles that are multiples of 64 (WGMMA). Selection heuristic: (1) compute average
  query length per batch (fused with head-group dimension for GQA), pick the *minimal* query
  tile ≥ it; (2) formulate register + shared-memory constraints as a function of KV tile size
  and maximize SM occupancy.
- **Wave quantization is the enemy.** The balanced scheduler (Algorithm 1) exists to keep SMs
  from idling on skewed KV lengths: cost model `cost(l_q, l_kv) = α·l_q + β·l_kv`; max KV chunk
  `L_kv = (Σ_i ⌈l_qo(i)/T_q⌉ · l_kv(i)) / #CTA`; split each query tile's KV into chunks of at
  most `L_kv`; greedily assign chunks to the currently cheapest CTA via a min-priority queue
  (a deterministic LPT-style Stream-K variant — atomics were *rejected* because serving needs
  bit-deterministic outputs).
- **#CTA is chosen as `k × #SM`, with `k ≤ 2` on Ampere and often `k = 1` on Hopper (a true
  persistent kernel).** Persistent + fixed grid is what makes graph capture and SM
  partitioning possible.
- **Explicit endorsement of SM partitioning for co-running kernels** (Appendix E): "Nanoflow
  overlaps GEMM, attention, and communication in separate CUDA streams, assigning a fixed
  number of SMs to each operation. **In FlashInfer, this SM number can be provided by the user
  through the plan functions, and the FlashInfer load-balancing scheduler will allocate tiles
  accordingly.**" This is exactly the cajeta submit-seam contract: the scheduler tells the
  kernel how much of the machine it owns, and the kernel's tile plan adapts.
- **Split-K writethrough**: only requests whose KV length exceeds `total_KV / #CTA` are split;
  short requests write directly to the final output, bypassing the workspace. Saves both
  workspace and contraction work.
- **Workspace is a first-class, pre-sized resource**: partial outputs bounded by
  `2 · #CTA × T_q × H_qo × (D+1)`; scheduler metadata sized from a user-declared upper bound
  on concurrent requests and max accumulated request length. Under graph capture the pointers
  must be *identical every step*, so the workspace is allocated at its worst case once.
- **CPU/GPU pipelining**: plan metadata is computed on a pinned host buffer and pushed with an
  async copy; the CPU plan for step *n* overlaps with GPU work.
- Backward pass is explicitly **not supported** — FlashInfer is inference-only; training
  needs customizable backward templates (stated as future work).

### 7.4 Metadata a scheduler needs (from this paper)

Per launch: query length per request `l_qo[i]`, KV length per request `l_kv[i]`, page/block
sizes `(B_r, B_c)`, head counts `H_qo`/`H_kv` (so `g` is derivable), head dim `D`, causal flag,
chosen query/KV tile sizes, `#CTA` budget (i.e. the SM allocation), workspace section base +
capacity, and the partial→final reduction map. Also a compile-time "attention variant" identity
so the JIT cache and the graph cache can key on it.

### 7.5 Testable requirements

1. When a kernel is submitted with per-item query length `l_qo` and KV length `l_kv`, the
   submit seam must carry both, and the arithmetic-character tag must be derived as
   `memory-bound if g·l_qo < crossover else compute-bound`, not asserted by the author.
2. When two kernels are co-scheduled, the seam must let each declare a compute-unit budget
   (`#CU` / `#SM`), and the tile plan must be recomputed against that budget rather than
   against the whole device.
3. When per-item KV lengths in a batch differ by more than 2×, the scheduler must split the
   long items' KV into chunks of at most `Σ⌈l_qo/T_q⌉·l_kv / #CU` and emit a partial-output
   reduction, or the launch must be rejected as unbalanced.
4. When a launch's grid size or workspace pointers would change between iterations, the
   scheduler must refuse to reuse a captured/graphed launch and must fall back to a fresh
   submit.
5. When a request's KV length is below `total_KV/#CU`, the scheduler must not split it
   (writethrough), and the partial-output workspace must not be sized for it.
6. When GQA group size `g > 1`, the tile planner must fuse the query-head dimension into the
   query-length dimension before selecting the query tile size.
7. When the plan inputs (per-request lengths) are unchanged from the previous step, `plan()`
   must be a cache hit and cost zero device work.
8. When the scheduler is asked to produce partial-output reductions, the reduction order must
   be deterministic (no atomics), and a repeated run with identical inputs must be bit-exact.

## 8. Miao et al. 2023/2025 — Towards Efficient Generative LLM Serving: A Survey from Algorithms to Systems (ACM CSUR)

A taxonomy paper, not a systems contribution. Its value here is (a) it names every technique
the cajeta scheduler will eventually have to accommodate, and (b) §3.2.5 "Kernel Optimization"
is essentially a checklist of what a kernel surface must expose.

### 8.1 Core mechanism

Two-branch taxonomy: **algorithmic innovations** (decoding algorithm — non-autoregressive,
speculative, early-exiting, cascade; architecture design — config downsizing, attention
simplification, recurrent units, activation sharing (MQA/GQA/MLA), conditional computing
(MoE); model compression — distillation, pruning) and **system optimizations** (low-bit
quantization; parallel computation — model/sequence parallelism, cloud scaling, decentralized;
memory management; request scheduling; kernel optimization — fusion, tailored attention,
variable sequence length, automatic compilation). §4 tabulates the frameworks
(FasterTransformer, vLLM, FlexFlow-Serve, TGI, LightLLM, MLC-LLM, TensorRT-LLM) against which
attention kernel they use for the **initial (prefill)** vs the **incremental (decode)** phase
and whether they optimize latency or throughput.

### 8.2 Key numbers / hardware facts worth carrying

- Hopper's FP8 tensor cores: **H100 reaches ~60× the TFLOPS of FP32**. Turing/Ampere added
  INT8/INT4. Quantization regimes in use: W8A16, W4A16 (GPTQ), W8A8 (SmoothQuant), W4A4.
- Structured pruning methods reach **50–60% sparsity** for LLMs; **2:4 and 4:8 semi-structured
  sparsity** maps to NVIDIA sparse tensor cores.
- The framework table (Table 2) shows the **industry consensus that prefill and decode use
  different kernels**: nearly every system pairs a `cuBLAS GEMM` / `xFormers` / `FlashAttention`
  *initial* kernel with a `paged attention` / `token attention` / `tree attention`
  *incremental* kernel. FlexFlow-Serve is the only one listed with tree attention (speculative).

### 8.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **Two kernel families per operator, selected by phase.** The survey's own table makes phase a
  first-class kernel-selection input: "initial" kernels parallelize over all prompt tokens;
  "incremental" kernels generate one token per iteration and parallelize over **batch × heads**,
  and further over the sequence dimension by partitioning the KV cache into chunks
  (FlashDecoding). FlashDecoding++ removes the partial-softmax synchronization by using a
  *unified maximum value known in advance* — i.e. an inter-kernel dependency that can be
  eliminated by a numeric contract, not by a barrier.
- **The decode kernel's parallelism is `batch × heads`, and that is often too small to fill a
  GPU.** This is the concrete reason decode is launch-/occupancy-bound at small batch and why
  splitting along the KV dimension (FlashDecoding) exists at all. A scheduler that can see
  `batch × heads × kv_chunks` can decide when to split-K.
- **Kernel fusion targets named**: (1) GEMMs with the same shape — the Q, K, V projections
  fuse into one GEMM; (2) add-bias fused with residual, layernorm, and activation. "Since the
  backward computation is not required for LLM inference, more kernel fusion chances exist."
  That is a direct statement that the *inference* schedule may fuse across boundaries the
  *training* schedule may not (backward needs the intermediates).
- **Variable sequence length is a first-class kernel problem**: padding to a common length
  wastes compute and memory; the named remedies are packing (contiguous, unpack before
  attention), **ragged tensors** (compiler-generated kernels with minimal padding), and
  bucketing into chunks. The survey explicitly says bucketing input prompts "brings new
  challenges to memory management and request scheduling" — chunking is not free at the
  scheduler level.
- **Memory management is scheduler-visible**: FasterTransformer pre-allocates a contiguous max-
  length buffer (waste on varied lengths and on parallel-sample decoding); vLLM pages;
  vAttention decouples virtual/physical with CUDA VMM to hide on-demand allocation latency;
  LightLLM goes token-level. The survey's warning is important for cajeta: "the overheads of
  such fragmented memory managing mechanisms pose new challenges. Especially for cases where
  other optimizations are employed to boost the batch size, these fine-grained memory
  management methods might offer only marginal throughput benefits while substantially
  amplifying the inference latency."
- **Request scheduling lineage**: Orca (iteration-level, FCFS) → continuous batching (vLLM,
  RayLLM), in-flight batching (TensorRT-LLM) → FastServe (JCT-aware preemption) → Sarathi-Serve
  (chunked prefill + piggybacked decodes) / DeepSpeed-FastGen "Dynamic SplitFuse" → S³ (output
  length prediction) → Splitwise/DistServe/ExeGPT (prefill-decode disaggregation) →
  TetriInfer, Andes, ConServe, Llumnix (mixed/heterogeneous workloads). For a *single-process*
  scheduler the relevant ones are Orca, Sarathi and FlashInfer; the rest are placement.
- **Compilers named as the alternative to vendor libraries**: TVM/Unity/Relax/TensorIR, MLIR,
  JAX, Triton, TASO, Mirage, TorchInductor — and the explicit motivation that a compiler
  approach "facilitates adaptation to alternative hardware platforms... and other types of GPUs
  (e.g., AMD GPUs and Apple M2 Ultra)." That is cajeta.xpu's thesis, stated by the survey.

### 8.4 Metadata a scheduler needs (from this paper)

Phase tag (initial/incremental), per-request true length + padding mask (or a ragged
descriptor), batch × head parallelism count, whether the KV layout is contiguous / paged /
token-level, quantization format of weights and activations (W-bits/A-bits), whether a fusion
boundary is legal (needs "is this intermediate required by a backward pass?"), and a
latency-vs-throughput optimization target per request.

### 8.5 Testable requirements

1. When an operator has both an initial-phase and an incremental-phase implementation, the
   submit seam must carry the phase tag and the dispatcher must select the implementation from
   it, never from a global mode flag.
2. When a batch's per-request lengths vary, the kernel must receive a ragged descriptor
   (offsets + true lengths) and must not be launched against a padded rectangle unless padding
   is explicitly requested.
3. When `batch × heads` is below the device's compute-unit count, the scheduler must consider a
   KV-dimension split (split-K / FlashDecoding) before accepting the launch.
4. When a kernel's intermediate is required by a backward pass, no fusion pass may eliminate
   its materialization.
5. When weights and activations carry different bit widths, the arithmetic-character tag must
   be computed from the *stored* bit width (bytes moved), not the compute bit width.
6. When a memory manager is paged or token-level, the scheduler must be able to report the
   per-launch indirection cost so a policy can decide whether the fragmentation win is real.

## 9. Dao et al. 2022 — FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness (NeurIPS'22)

### 9.1 Core mechanism

Attention is memory-bound, not compute-bound, because the standard implementation
materializes `S = QKᵀ` and `P = softmax(S)` (both `N×N`) in HBM. FlashAttention tiles Q, K, V
so that a block of K,V is loaded once into SRAM and iterated against all blocks of Q, computing
the softmax incrementally via the **online-softmax running statistics `(m, ℓ)`** (running max
and running sum of exponentials) so the full row of `S` is never needed at once. The entire
chain — matmul, mask, softmax, dropout, matmul — is fused into **one CUDA kernel**. For the
backward pass, `S` and `P` are **recomputed** on-chip from Q,K,V and the saved `(m, ℓ)` rather
than reloaded from HBM: more FLOPs, far fewer HBM accesses, net faster. Block sizes are chosen
from SRAM capacity: `B_c = ⌈M/4d⌉`, `B_r = min(⌈M/4d⌉, d)`.

### 9.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- **Memory hierarchy used as the model** (A100): SRAM ≈ 19 TB/s, 20 MB aggregate (192 KB per
  SM × 108 SMs); HBM 1.5–2.0 TB/s, 40–80 GB; CPU DRAM 12.8 GB/s, >1 TB.
- **The decisive table** (GPT-2 medium, seq 1024, head dim 64, 16 heads, batch 64, A100):

  | | Standard | FlashAttention |
  |---|---|---|
  | GFLOPs | 66.6 | **75.2** (+13%) |
  | HBM R/W (GB) | 40.3 | **4.4** (−9.2×) |
  | Runtime (ms) | 41.7 | **7.3** (−5.7×) |

  *More FLOPs, less time.* This is the empirical proof that for this operator the roofline is
  the memory axis, and it is the number to quote when justifying a memory-bound tag.
- IO complexity: standard attention is `Θ(Nd + N²)` HBM accesses; FlashAttention is
  `Θ(N²d²M⁻¹)` where `M` is SRAM size — and this is proven **optimal** (Proposition 3: no exact
  attention algorithm achieves `o(N²d²M⁻¹)` for `M ∈ [d, Nd]`). For `d = 64–128` and
  `M ≈ 100 KB`, `d² ≪ M`, so the reduction is large.
- Block-sparse variant: `Θ(Nd + N²d²M⁻¹s)` with `s` the nonzero-block fraction; **2.8×** on LRA,
  **2–4×** faster than dense FlashAttention, scales to 64K.
- **Block-size sweep** (forward, GPT-2 medium, A100): HBM accesses fall monotonically with block
  size from 64→512, but runtime falls only up to **block size ≈ 256**; beyond that the kernel is
  bottlenecked by arithmetic, and larger blocks stop fitting in SRAM. *There is a measurable
  optimum, and it is where the operator flips from memory-bound to compute-bound.*
- End-to-end: BERT-large MLPerf 1.1 record 20.0 ± 1.5 min → **17.4 ± 1.4 min (15% faster)**,
  8×A100. GPT-2 small: HuggingFace 9.5 d, Megatron-LM 4.7 d, FlashAttention **2.7 d**
  (3.5× / 1.7×). GPT-2 medium: 21.0 / 11.5 / **6.9 d**. Attention computation alone **7.6×**.
- Longer context for free: GPT-2 small at **4K context takes 3.6 d — still 30% faster than
  Megatron at 1K context (4.7 d)** — with 0.7 better perplexity.
- **Crossover with approximate attention**: FlashAttention is faster than *exact* baselines at
  all lengths (up to 3× vs PyTorch), and approximate/sparse methods (Linformer, Performer,
  Reformer…) only begin to cross over with it at **sequence lengths between 512 and 1024**.
  Below 1K, approximation buys nothing.
- Memory footprint grows linearly in `N`; **20× smaller** than exact baselines and still **2×
  smaller than Linformer** at 64K.

### 9.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **Classification rule, stated in the paper's own terms**: an operation is compute-bound if
  time is set by arithmetic (matmul with large inner dimension, convolution with many channels)
  and memory-bound if time is set by accesses (elementwise ops — activation, dropout;
  reductions — sum, softmax, batch/layer norm). Every non-GEMM kernel in a transformer is in
  the second class. A scheduler that only knows "this is attention" knows nothing; it needs the
  shape.
- **The fused attention kernel is a single indivisible unit.** It cannot be split into
  matmul/softmax/matmul without recreating the 40.3 GB of HBM traffic. So the cooperative-tile
  surface must be able to express a whole fused loop nest as one submit, with the `(m, ℓ)`
  running statistics living in registers/LDS across the inner loop — this is precisely the
  `Group/Tile/mac/reduce` shape.
- **Recomputation is a scheduling decision with a memory/compute trade.** In training, the
  backward kernel's read set is `{Q, K, V, O, dO, L}` — *not* `{S, P}`. That changes the
  scheduler's liveness analysis: the `N×N` intermediates never exist, so a co-running kernel can
  use that HBM. The tag must therefore be per-kernel, not per-operator.
- **Explicit statement that naive fusion breaks under training**: "in the context of model
  training, the intermediate values still need to be written to HBM to save for the backward
  pass, reducing the effectiveness of naive kernel fusion." Fusion legality depends on whether a
  backward pass will consume the intermediate — a compiler-visible fact the scheduler needs.
- **The paper's own listed limitation is cajeta's thesis**: "Our current approach to building
  IO-aware implementations of attention requires writing a new CUDA kernel for each new
  attention implementation… Implementations may also not be transferrable across GPU
  architectures. These limitations suggest the need for a method that supports writing attention
  algorithms in a high-level language… and compiling to IO-aware implementations in CUDA."

### 9.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Sequence length `N`, head dim `d`, head count, batch, the **SRAM/LDS budget `M`** of the target
(block sizes are a function of it), whether a causal/sparse mask applies and its block pattern,
whether the backward pass will need this kernel's output statistics, measured HBM bytes moved
(so the scheduler can compute achieved bandwidth, not guess), and the arithmetic-intensity
estimate `FLOPs / bytes`.

### 9.5 Testable requirements

1. When a kernel declares its tile shape, the compiler must reject or re-tile any configuration
   whose per-group LDS/register footprint exceeds the target's `M`, rather than emitting a
   kernel that spills or fails to launch.
2. When the scheduler estimates a kernel's arithmetic character, it must use FLOPs/byte computed
   from the declared read+write sets and shapes; a kernel with more FLOPs than an alternative may
   still be classified memory-bound and must be allowed to win.
3. When a fused loop nest carries loop-carried scalar state (running max, running sum), the tile
   surface must keep that state in registers across the inner loop and must not force a
   round-trip to global memory between tiles.
4. When a kernel's intermediate is not consumed by any backward pass in the program, the
   compiler must be permitted to elide its materialization and the scheduler must see the
   reduced write set.
5. When block size is swept, the runtime must expose measured HBM bytes and achieved bandwidth
   per launch, so the memory-bound/compute-bound flip point can be found empirically rather than
   declared.
6. When the sequence length is below 1024, the scheduler must prefer the dense exact kernel over
   any block-sparse variant (the crossover is measured at 512–1024).

## 10. Dao 2023 — FlashAttention-2: Faster Attention with Better Parallelism and Work Partitioning

### 10.1 Core mechanism

FlashAttention-1 was IO-optimal but still only reached 30–50% of peak FLOPs/s forward and
25–35% backward, while a tuned GEMM reaches 80–90%. FA2 fixes three *scheduling* problems, not
IO problems. (1) **Minimize non-matmul FLOPs**: keep an unscaled output `Õ` through the inner
loop and rescale once at the end, and store only the logsumexp `L = m + log(ℓ)` instead of both
`m` and `ℓ`. (2) **Add sequence-length parallelism**: swap the loop order so the *outer* loop is
over query row blocks, making the forward pass embarrassingly parallel over row blocks — one
threadblock per (row block, head, batch) instead of one per (head, batch). The backward pass is
parallelized over *column* blocks, with atomic adds for the shared `dQ` update. (3) **Re-partition
work between warps**: FA1 split K,V across the 4 warps of a block ("split-K") which forced every
warp to stage partial `QKᵀ` results through shared memory and synchronize; FA2 splits **Q** across
warps and keeps K,V visible to all warps, eliminating inter-warp communication in the forward pass.

### 10.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- **A100 peak: 312 TFLOPs/s FP16/BF16 matmul vs 19.5 TFLOPs/s non-matmul FP32 → one non-matmul
  FLOP costs as much as ~16 matmul FLOPs.** This is the single most quotable constant for a
  cooperative-tile design: a `mac` on tensor/matrix cores and a `reduce` in the ALU are not the
  same currency.
- FA2 on A100 80GB SXM4: **~2× FlashAttention**, 1.3–1.5× FlashAttention-Triton forward and ~2×
  backward, **3–10× vs standard PyTorch attention**. Reaches **up to 230 TFLOPs/s = 73% of A100
  theoretical max** forward, up to 63% backward.
- Attention fwd+bwd, A100, causal, head dim 128, seq 16k: PyTorch ~34, FlashAttention ~83,
  xformers ~93, FA-Triton ~(OOM), **FA2 189 TFLOPs/s**. Non-causal head dim 128 seq 16k:
  **FA2 203 TFLOPs/s** vs FA 83.
- End-to-end GPT training on **8×A100 80GB SXM** (TFLOPs/s per GPU):

  | Model | no FA | FA | FA2 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | GPT3-1.3B, 2k ctx | 142 | 189 | **196** |
  | GPT3-1.3B, 8k ctx | 72 | 170 | **220** |
  | GPT3-2.7B, 2k ctx | 149 | 189 | **205** |
  | GPT3-2.7B, 8k ctx | 80 | 175 | **225** (72% MFU) |

  Note the shape of this table: at **8k context the un-optimized baseline loses half its
  throughput (142→72, 149→80)** while FA2 *gains* (196→220, 205→225). Long context is where
  scheduling quality decides the run.
- Same code on **H100 with no new instructions: up to 335 TFLOPs/s**; the author expects another
  1.5–2× from TMA and 4th-gen tensor cores.
- **Causal masking is worth 1.7–1.8×** by skipping whole blocks whose column indices exceed row
  indices (about half the blocks at long sequence length), and by applying the mask to only one
  block per row.
- **Occupancy threshold, stated numerically**: FA1's `batch × heads` threadblock count "is
  efficient when this number is large (say ≥ 80), since we can effectively use almost all of the
  compute resources" on an A100's **108 SMs**. Below that, sequence-length parallelism is
  required. This is the concrete criterion for when a decode/small-batch launch under-fills.
- Backward pass has **5 matmuls vs 2 in the forward**, so backward FLOPs ≈ 2.5× forward.
  FLOPs formula used: forward `= 4 · seqlen² · head_dim · num_heads` (halve for causal).
- Practical block sizes: `{64,128} × {64,128}` chosen per head dimension and device shared
  memory. "Past a certain block size, register spilling causes significant slowdown, or the
  amount of shared memory required is larger than what the GPU has available, **and the kernel
  cannot run at all**." Only ~4 choices, tuned manually; auto-tuning left as future work.
- Benchmark setup worth copying: **fix total tokens at 16k** and vary seqlen 512…16k with batch
  adjusted accordingly, hidden 2048, head dim 64 or 128.

### 10.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **The parallelism axis must be selectable at submit time.** `batch × heads` is the natural
  axis; when it drops below ~0.75× the compute-unit count, the scheduler must add a
  sequence-length (or KV-chunk) axis. This is the same decision FlashInfer's split-K and
  FlashDecoding make, arrived at independently. For a gfx1151-class APU with far fewer CUs than
  an A100's 108, the threshold is proportionally lower but the rule is identical: *count the
  workgroups the launch will produce and compare it to the CU count.*
- **Forward and backward have different parallel axes and different sharing.** Forward: parallel
  over row blocks, no cross-block communication → trivially co-schedulable and splittable.
  Backward: parallel over column blocks with **atomic accumulation into `dQ`** → a write-set
  conflict that forbids naive co-running of two backward kernels over the same `dQ`, and makes
  the backward launch non-deterministic in float unless ordered. The submit seam's write set
  must distinguish *exclusive write* from *accumulating write*.
- **Non-matmul FLOPs are a scheduler-visible cost class.** With a 16:1 cost ratio, an
  arithmetic-character tag that is a single bit (compute vs memory) is not enough for a
  cooperative-tile surface; the useful decomposition is (matrix-core FLOPs, vector-ALU FLOPs,
  bytes). A kernel that is "compute bound" on vector ALU is scheduled very differently from one
  bound on matrix cores — the two can in principle co-run.
- **Warp/lane-level work partitioning is a correctness-and-performance property of the tile
  abstraction, not of the kernel body.** FA1 vs FA2 differ only in which operand is split across
  warps; the algorithm is identical. A `Group`/`Tile` surface must therefore let the author (or
  the compiler) choose *which* operand is subgroup-private and which is group-shared, because
  that choice was worth 2×.
- **Register/LDS pressure is a hard launch failure, not a slowdown.** The paper says the kernel
  "cannot run at all" past a block size. The compiler must model the budget and refuse, and the
  scheduler must have the fallback tile shape available. (This matches cajeta's own recorded
  finding that halving accumulators won 55–70% once spilling was visible.)
- **Causal masking changes the work distribution, not just the work.** Skipping upper-triangle
  blocks makes per-row-block cost linear in the row index — an inherently *imbalanced* launch.
  Static equal partitioning across CUs wastes up to 2×; the scheduler needs the mask pattern to
  balance (this is the same load-imbalance FlashInfer's cost model addresses).

### 10.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per launch: matmul-FLOPs and non-matmul-FLOPs separately; bytes read/written; workgroup count
that the chosen tiling will produce (to compare against CU count); per-group register and LDS
footprint; which operand is group-shared vs subgroup-private; mask pattern (dense / causal /
block-sparse with its block table); whether any write is an accumulation (atomic) rather than an
exclusive store; and for training, whether this launch is forward or backward (different axis,
different sharing, ×2.5 FLOPs).

### 10.5 Testable requirements

1. When the workgroup count implied by a tiling is below the device's compute-unit count, the
   scheduler must select a tiling that adds a reduction axis (sequence/KV split) before
   launching.
2. When a kernel's per-group register + LDS footprint exceeds the target's budget, the compiler
   must fail the tiling at compile time and select the next smaller tile, never emit a kernel
   that cannot launch.
3. When a launch declares an accumulating (atomic) write set, the scheduler must not co-run it
   with any other launch that writes the same buffer, and must mark the launch's result as
   order-dependent.
4. When a kernel is tagged with an arithmetic character, that tag must decompose into
   matrix-core FLOPs, vector-ALU FLOPs, and bytes, since the device peaks for the first two
   differ by ~16×.
5. When a causal or block-sparse mask is declared, the scheduler must use the mask's nonzero
   block count per row-block for load balancing rather than assuming uniform tiles.
6. When the same operator is submitted as forward and as backward, the two must be allowed
   different tile shapes and different parallel axes.
7. When a tile shape is changed, a regression test must confirm the achieved fraction of device
   peak did not fall (FA1→FA2 is a 30–50% → 50–73% of peak change with identical math).

## 11. Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer 2021/2022 — Switch Transformers (JMLR 23)

### 11.1 Core mechanism

Replace the dense FFN in a Transformer block with a **Switch FFN layer**: a router
`h(x) = W_r·x` → softmax over `N` experts, and each token is dispatched to **exactly one**
expert (`k = 1`), against the prior belief that `k ≥ 2` was needed for useful router
gradients. Output is `p_i(x)·E_i(x)`, so the gate value keeps the router differentiable.
Because tensor shapes must be static, each expert gets a fixed **expert capacity**
`C = (tokens_per_batch / num_experts) × capacity_factor`; tokens beyond an expert's capacity
are **dropped** (passed through the residual, skipping the layer). A differentiable auxiliary
load-balancing loss `loss = α·N·Σ_i f_i·P_i` (`f_i` = fraction of tokens dispatched to expert
`i`, `P_i` = mean router probability for `i`, `α = 10⁻²`) pushes both vectors toward `1/N`.
Stability at scale comes from **selective precision** — the router's logits/softmax are cast
to float32 *locally*, and the dispatch/combine tensors are cast back to bfloat16 *before*
they cross the all-to-all.

### 11.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

All on **32 TPUv3 cores**, FLOP-matched to T5-Base.

| Model | Capacity factor | Time-to-quality (h) | Examples/sec |
|---|---|---|---|
| T5-Base | — | never reached | 1600 |
| T5-Large | — | 131.1 | 470 |
| Switch-Base | 1.0 | **62.8** | **1000** |
| Switch-Base | 1.25 | 65.0 | 910 |
| Switch-Base | 2.0 | 72.8 | 860 |
| MoE-Base (top-2) | 1.0 | 80.1 | 860 |
| MoE-Base (top-2) | 1.25 | 80.7 | 790 |
| MoE-Base (top-2) | 2.0 | 68.7 | 840 |

- **Capacity factor is a throughput knob with a ~14% swing** (1000 → 860 examples/sec going
  1.0 → 2.0 on Switch-Base). Note the anomaly the authors flag: MoE-Base got *slower* going
  from CF 2.0 to 1.25 (840 → 790) — the shape change interacted badly with the implementation.
  Capacity factor is not monotone in wall clock; it must be measured.
- Dropped tokens are **typically < 1%** with the aux loss at `α = 10⁻²`, and they showed no
  dependence on the number of experts.
- **Selective precision**: full float32 = 1160 examples/sec, quality −1.718. Pure bfloat16 =
  **1390 examples/sec but diverged** (−3.780). Selective (router local region in fp32) = 1390
  examples/sec, quality −1.716. **≈20% throughput recovered by confining the fp32 region so no
  fp32 tensor crosses the all-to-all.**
- Speedups at equal FLOPs/token: **7×** (Switch-Base 64 experts vs T5-Base), **2.5×** vs
  T5-Large (which uses 3.5× more FLOPs/token), **4×** for Switch-C (1.571 T params, 2048
  experts, 890 B FLOPs/seq) vs T5-XXL. Multilingual: mean **5×** step speedup, **91% of 101
  languages ≥ 4×**.
- Router cost is `O(d_model × num_experts)` — negligible; the number of experts is the *cheap*
  scaling axis.
- Switch-XXL (395 B params, 6.3 T FLOPs/seq, 64 experts, experts every other layer) is
  **sometimes unstable**; Switch-C (1.6 T, 2048 experts, ~10× fewer FLOPs/token) showed no
  instability. Instability tracks FLOPs/token, not parameter count.
- Distillation: 99% compression retains ~28–30% of the sparse model's quality gain.

### 11.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **MoE turns one large dense GEMM into `E` small GEMMs of ragged, data-dependent shape.**
  Even with a static capacity `C`, the *useful* rows per expert vary per step; padding to `C`
  is wasted compute that scales with `capacity_factor`. On one GPU in one process, the
  scheduler sees: one tiny router GEMM (`[T, d_model] × [d_model, N]`, memory-bound, launch-
  overhead-dominated), one gather/scatter (pure memory movement, `[n, B/n, E, C]` dispatch
  matrix), `E` expert GEMMs, and one combine/scatter-back.
- The dispatch is expressed as `einsum([n, B/n, d_model], [n, B/n, E, C], dim=[B/n])` producing
  `[n, E, C, d_model]` — i.e. **a one-hot matmul used as a gather**. On a single GPU this is
  the wrong lowering; it is a permutation and should be a gather kernel, but it is the shape
  the scheduler will be handed if a port copies the reference.
- **The expert GEMMs are mutually independent and are the natural co-run candidates.** They
  have identical weights-shape but different row counts. With top-1 routing and `C` capacity
  each is `[C, d_model] × [d_model, d_ff]` — at decode-time batch sizes `C` is *tiny* (e.g.
  `B=8` tokens over `N=64` experts ⇒ `C≈0`), so MoE decode is a swarm of launch-bound,
  memory-bound skinny GEMMs. This is the single worst case for launch overhead in the whole
  workload class and the strongest argument for grouped/batched-GEMM (one launch, per-group
  offsets) plus co-scheduling.
- **Load imbalance is a scheduler-visible runtime property**, not a static one. `f_i` (the
  realized token count per expert) is known *after* the router runs and *before* the expert
  GEMMs launch. A single-process scheduler can read it and size the expert launches to the
  actual counts (no capacity padding at inference), which is strictly better than the TPU
  static-shape design that forced capacity factors.
- **Precision boundaries are placement constraints**: the fp32 region must be *local* to the
  router kernel; a scheduler that fuses or reorders across the router boundary must not widen
  the fp32 region, or it recreates the 1390→1160 examples/sec loss.
- Serialization: router → dispatch → experts → combine is a hard chain per MoE layer. Only the
  `E` expert kernels are parallel with each other. Attention of layer `L+1` cannot start before
  the combine of layer `L`. The overlap opportunity is *across requests/microbatches*, not
  within one layer.

### 11.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per MoE launch: number of experts `E`, realized per-expert token counts `f_i·T` (device-side,
needs to be readable or usable as an indirect launch bound), capacity `C` and whether capacity
is enforced, the dispatch index array, per-expert weight buffer handles (read set), the
combine output buffer (write set), and a precision annotation marking the router region as
fp32-local. For training, additionally the aux-loss scalar output (a tiny reduction that must
not serialize the pipeline).

### 11.5 Testable requirements

1. When an MoE layer is submitted, the scheduler must receive `E` independent expert launches
   with per-expert row counts as a runtime buffer, and must be able to issue them as one
   grouped launch rather than `E` separate launches.
2. When per-expert row counts are available on-device, the launch bound must be taken from
   that buffer (indirect dispatch), not from a host-side capacity constant.
3. When a router kernel is tagged fp32-local, no fusion or tiling pass may extend fp32 across
   the dispatch boundary.
4. When expert row counts are imbalanced by more than a configured factor, the scheduler must
   report the imbalance (it is the direct analogue of the `< 1%` dropped-token metric) rather
   than silently padding.
5. When two MoE expert GEMMs have disjoint read sets and disjoint write regions, the scheduler
   must be permitted to co-run them.
6. When an MoE layer's combine has not completed, no kernel of the next layer that reads the
   combine output may be scheduled.
7. When the batch is a decode step (few tokens, many experts), the scheduler must classify the
   expert GEMMs as launch-bound/memory-bound and prefer a single fused launch over `E` launches.

## 12. Shoeybi et al. 2019/2020 — Megatron-LM: Training Multi-Billion Parameter Language Models Using Model Parallelism

The only *training* paper in the set. Its value here is the **overlap and duplication rules**, and
its explicit design goal of "keeping the GPUs compute bound."

### 12.1 Core mechanism

Intra-layer (tensor) model parallelism, implemented with two conjugate primitives `f` and `g`
inserted into an otherwise unmodified PyTorch transformer — no compiler, no framework rewrite.
For the MLP `Y = GeLU(XA); Z = Dropout(YB)`: split `A` **column-wise** (`A = [A₁, A₂]`) so GeLU
applies independently to each partition (splitting `A` row-wise would force a synchronization
*before* the nonlinearity, because `GeLU(X₁A₁ + X₂A₂) ≠ GeLU(X₁A₁) + GeLU(X₂A₂)`), then split
`B` **row-wise** so the second GEMM consumes the first's local output directly. For self-attention,
partition the Q/K/V GEMMs column-wise **by attention head**, so each head's matmul is local, and
partition the output linear row-wise. Each block therefore needs **exactly one all-reduce in the
forward (`g`) and one in the backward (`f`)** — 4 collectives per transformer layer per
fwd+bwd. Layernorm, dropout, and residual are **duplicated on every worker rather than
partitioned and broadcast**. The output-embedding GEMM is fused with the cross-entropy loss so the
all-gather communicates `b × s` scalars instead of `b × s × v` logits.

### 12.2 Key numbers (hardware named)

- Hardware: up to 32 DGX-2H servers = **512 Tesla V100 SXM3 32 GB**; **300 GB/s** between GPUs
  inside a server via NVSwitch, **100 GB/s** between servers over 8 InfiniBand adapters per server.
- Single-GPU baseline: 1.2 B parameters sustains **39 TFLOPs = 30% of theoretical peak** on one
  V100. 8.3 B on 512 GPUs with 8-way tensor parallel + 64-way data parallel sustains
  **15.1 PFLOPs = 76% scaling efficiency**.
- Weak-scaling efficiency (Figure 5): tensor-parallel only at 1/2/4/8 GPUs = **100 / 95 / 83 /
  77%**; +data parallel at 64/128/256/512 GPUs = **96 / 83 / 79 / 74%**.
- Scaling configs (Table 1): hidden 1536/1920/2304/3072, heads 16/20/24/32 (**hidden size per head
  held constant at 96**), layers 40/54/64/72, params 1.2/2.5/4.2/8.3 B.
- **Tile quantization, stated explicitly**: "The original vocabulary size was 50,257, however, to
  have efficient GEMMs for the logit layer, it is beneficial for the **per-GPU vocabulary size to
  be a multiple of 128**. Since we study up to 8-way model parallelism, we pad the vocabulary so
  that it is divisible by 128 × 8 = 1024, resulting in a padded vocabulary size of 51,200."
- Training cost (Table 2): 8.3 B GPT-2 (72 layers, hidden 3072, 24 heads, 128/head) on 512 GPUs
  = **2.10 days/epoch**; 2.5 B on 128 GPUs = 2.27 d; 355 M on 64 GPUs = 0.86 d.
- Memory technique: **activation checkpointing after every transformer layer** (recompute
  activations in the backward pass).

### 12.3 Kernel-level scheduling implications

- **The central rule, in the authors' words**: "Much of our model parallel approach can be
  characterized as techniques aimed at **reducing communication and keeping the GPUs compute
  bound**. Rather than having one GPU compute part of the dropout, layer normalization, or
  residual connections and broadcast the results to other GPUs, we choose to **duplicate the
  computation across GPUs**." Generalized for a single-GPU, single-process scheduler: *when a
  memory-bound elementwise/reduction op is cheaper to recompute than to synchronize around,
  recompute it.* Same shape as FlashAttention's `S`/`P` recomputation and vLLM's
  recompute-vs-swap choice. **Recompute-vs-communicate is a first-class scheduler decision and
  needs a cost model on both sides.**
- **Where the synchronization points go is a *layout* decision, and layout is what removes them.**
  Column-split-then-row-split fuses two GEMMs with no sync between them precisely because the
  nonlinearity between them is elementwise. That is exactly the reasoning a cooperative-tile
  compiler must do: *which operand axis is partitioned determines whether the next op needs a
  cross-group reduction.* Megatron's `f`/`g` pair is the tensor-parallel analogue of FA2's
  choice to split Q rather than K,V across warps.
- **Reduce collectives to their smallest carrier.** Fusing the logit GEMM with cross-entropy
  turns a `b × s × v` all-gather into a `b × s` all-reduce (v = 51,200, so ~50,000× less traffic).
  In-process the analogue is fusing a producer with its consuming reduction so the large
  intermediate never leaves registers/LDS.
- **Gradient reduction overlaps with backward compute, and multiple reductions run in parallel**:
  "During back propagation we run **multiple gradient all-reduces in parallel** to reduce weight
  gradients within each distinct data parallel group." So the backward pass is a *pipeline*: as
  each layer's `dW` becomes available, its reduction is launched and runs concurrently with the
  next layer's `dX` computation. For a cajeta single-process scheduler with an XPU backend, the
  equivalent is: **a reduction (or a DMA to host / peer) issued on a separate queue must be able
  to run concurrently with the next backward kernel**, and the scheduler must track the
  per-parameter readiness rather than waiting for the whole backward.
- **RNG state is a scheduling hazard.** "Because some dropout layers are in a model parallel
  region, while others are not, we need to treat random number generation carefully… To
  synchronize residual connection dropout across model parallel workers we seed the RNGs at the
  beginning of training with the same seed… However, dropout within a model parallel region should
  result in different random numbers." Two distinct RNG streams, and which one a kernel uses is a
  correctness property the scheduler must not permute. Any cajeta kernel that consumes randomness
  needs an explicit stream identity in its metadata, or a rescheduling changes the model.
- **Activation checkpointing changes the read/write sets per layer**: forward writes only layer
  boundaries; backward re-runs the forward of a layer before consuming it. So a checkpointed
  layer's backward is *two* launches with a transient buffer, and its peak residency is one
  layer's activations, not the whole model's. The scheduler must see recompute as a real launch
  with real cost, not as free.
- **30% of peak on a single GPU is the honest baseline for training.** The paper calls 39 TFLOPs
  "a strong single GPU baseline" at 30% of a V100's peak. Compare FA2's 50–73% for attention alone
  and 72% MFU end-to-end on A100. **Training MFU targets belong in the 30–72% band**, and a cajeta
  training path should be measured against that, not against 100%.

### 12.4 Metadata a scheduler needs

Per launch: which axis of each operand is partitioned (so the next op's need for a cross-group
reduction is derivable); whether the op is elementwise (duplicable) or reducing (needs a
collective); the RNG stream identity; whether this is a recompute launch and what it re-derives;
per-parameter gradient readiness so a reduction can be launched as soon as a `dW` is complete; and
a padding/tile granularity for every GEMM dimension (Megatron's 128 multiple).

### 12.5 Testable requirements

1. When a GEMM's dimension is not a multiple of the target's tile granularity, the compiler must
   pad (or report) rather than emit an inefficient kernel; a test must show the padded and
   unpadded shapes differ in measured time by more than a stated threshold.
2. When a gradient buffer for parameter `p` becomes complete during the backward pass, its
   reduction/transfer must be launchable immediately and must run concurrently with the next
   backward kernel; a test must show overlap, not serialization.
3. When an elementwise op sits between two partitioned GEMMs, the scheduler must choose the
   partition axes so that no synchronization is required between them, or report why it cannot.
4. When a kernel consumes randomness, its RNG stream identity must be part of the launch metadata,
   and reordering launches must not change which stream a kernel draws from.
5. When activation checkpointing is enabled, the recompute launch must appear in the schedule with
   its own cost and its own buffer reservation, and the peak residency must be measurably lower
   than the non-checkpointed run.
6. When a reduction can be fused into its producer (e.g. logits + cross-entropy), the fused form
   must be preferred and the intermediate must not be materialized.
7. When a memory-bound op would otherwise require a synchronization, the scheduler must be able to
   choose duplication/recomputation, and the choice must be driven by measured costs on both sides.

# 13. Synthesis for the ML workload class

## 13.1 The phase model and the exact per-kernel roofline classes

### 13.1.1 The one algebraic fact that organizes everything

For a **weight-stationary GEMM** `[T, d] × [d, k]` with `T` tokens and weights stored at `b_w`
bytes/element, with `T ≪ d, k` so weight traffic dominates:

```
FLOPs      = 2·T·d·k
bytes      ≈ d·k·b_w
intensity  = 2·T / b_w        [FLOP/byte]        (derived)
```

Let `R = (device dense matrix FLOPS) / (achieved HBM bandwidth)`. The kernel is memory-bound
while `2T/b_w < R`, i.e. below

```
T_crossover = R · b_w / 2                        (derived)
```

**Three consequences, all load-bearing:**

1. **The roofline coordinate is total tokens in the launch, not batch size and not requests.**
   Sarathi says this in words ("the cost of the linear operation for 1 decode token is nearly the
   same as 128 prefill tokens"); DistServe says it as an engineering rule ("the number of new
   tokens in the batch is a reliable indicator of the batch's real execution time").
2. **The derivation matches the measurements.** A100-40GB: `R = 312 TFLOP/s ÷ 1.555 TB/s ≈ 200`,
   and with fp16 weights (`b_w = 2`) `T_crossover ≈ 200` — exactly Sarathi's stated theoretical
   figure ("we expect the operators to become compute-bound at ~200 tokens on A100 GPUs"). Their
   *measured* figure is **500–600 tokens** at TP-4, the gap being fixed per-launch overheads and
   collectives. DistServe independently measures `L_m ≈ 512` tokens for a 13B on one A100.
   **Rule: the practical crossover is 2.5–3× the algebraic one; measure it, don't compute it.**
3. **Quantization moves the crossover DOWN, not up.** With 4-bit weights (`b_w = 0.5`),
   `T_crossover = R/4`. On an A100 that is ~50 tokens algebraically, perhaps ~130–150 in practice.
   For cajeta-llm's quantized matvec kernels this is the most important derived result in this
   document: **the "free compute" that chunked prefill and speculative decoding spend does not
   exist in the same quantity for a Q4/MXFP4 model as for an fp16 one**, because the memory side
   already got 4× cheaper. Any policy tuned against fp16 literature will over-admit work.

### 13.1.2 Attention is a different operator in each phase — by three orders of magnitude

FlashAttention's traffic is `Θ(N²d²/M)` for `4N²d` FLOPs, so **prefill attention intensity ≈
`4M/d`** — with `M ≈ 100 KB` of SRAM/LDS and `d = 128` that is **≈3000 FLOP/byte**, i.e.
compute-bound by roughly 20× on any current device. Sarathi confirms empirically: "even at small
chunk sizes the attention prefill operation is compute bound."

**Decode attention** reads the whole KV cache to serve `g` query heads (GQA group size) per KV
head: `FLOPs = 4·L·d·g`, `bytes = 2·L·d·b_kv`, so **intensity ≈ `2g/b_kv`** — for fp16 KV and
`g = 8`, **8 FLOP/byte**, memory-bound by 20–40×. FlashInfer states the general form:
`I = O(1/(1/l_qo + 1/l_kv))`, which in serving simplifies to **`O(g · l_qo)`** — *query length
alone*, and **batching does not change it**.

So the **same operator swings ~400× in arithmetic intensity** purely from `l_qo`. A submit seam
that carries only "this is attention" cannot classify it; one that carries `(l_qo, l_kv, g)` gets
it for free, and gets speculative decoding's `l_qo = γ+1` right without a special case.

### 13.1.3 Per-kernel class table for one decoder layer

| # | Kernel | Prefill (T = chunk, e.g. 512–2048) | Decode (T = batch, e.g. 1–64) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | RMSNorm / LayerNorm | memory-bound | memory-bound, **launch-bound** | fuse into neighbour |
| 2 | QKV projection GEMM | **compute** above `T_crossover` | **memory** | ~80% of layer time (Sarathi) |
| 3 | RoPE | memory | memory, launch-bound | fusing into attention: **1.6–3.7× BW util** (FlashInfer) |
| 4 | KV-cache scatter write | memory (pure) | memory (pure), tiny | fuse with reshape (vLLM kernel #1) |
| 5 | Attention | **compute**, ≈3000 FLOP/byte | **memory**, ≈`2g/b_kv` ≈ 8 | the 400× swing |
| 6 | Attn out projection GEMM | compute | memory | same rule as #2 |
| 7 | MLP gate/up GEMM | compute | memory | largest weights |
| 8 | SwiGLU / GeLU | memory | memory, launch-bound | Megatron: duplicate, don't communicate |
| 9 | MLP down GEMM | compute | memory | |
| 10 | Residual adds | memory | launch-bound | |
| 11 | (MoE) router GEMM | memory | memory, launch-bound | `O(d·E)`, negligible FLOPs |
| 12 | (MoE) dispatch gather / combine scatter | memory (pure) | memory, launch-bound | permutation, not a matmul |
| 13 | (MoE) `E` expert GEMMs | compute if `T·k/E > T_crossover` | **launch-bound swarm** | see §13.4 |
| 14 | LM head `[T,d]×[d,V]` | compute | **memory**, biggest single weight read | `V` = 32K–150K |
| 15 | Sampling / argmax | memory | launch-bound, µs-scale | |

**Phase summary with the measured evidence:**

- **Prefill = compute-bound, saturates at batch ≈ 1–2.** Splitwise: prompt-phase throughput
  *decreases* after 2048 prompt tokens. DistServe Fig. 3: prefill throughput is flat in batch size
  for inputs ≥ 512. Sarathi Fig. 3: prefill throughput saturates at batch size 1 (~4–5K tok/s,
  Mistral-7B, A100). Splitwise: prompt-phase power rises toward TDP and prefill latency is highly
  sensitive to power caps.
- **Decode = memory-bound, scales ~linearly with batch until residency runs out.** Sarathi:
  ≈10 → 100 → 200 → 420 → 800 tok/s at batch 1/8/16/32/64. Splitwise: "with a batch size of 64,
  there is only 2× impact on TBT" — 64× the work for 2× the time. Splitwise: token-phase latency
  is *unchanged* by a >50% power cap (700 W → 350 W). A100→H100 (3.43× compute, 1.64× bandwidth)
  buys prefill 0.51× TTFT but decode only 0.70× TBT: **decode tracks the bandwidth ratio.**
- **Decode does eventually become compute-bound.** DistServe: "as the decoding batch size continues
  to increase, the decoding computation begins to resemble the prefill phase"; paged attention and
  GQA "enable further scaling of the decoding batch size to nearly compute-bound." The crossover is
  `T_crossover` from §13.1.1 applied to the batch: **fp16 weights ⇒ ~200 (A100 theory) / ~500–600
  (measured, TP-4); 4-bit weights ⇒ roughly a quarter of that.**

## 13.2 Chunked prefill and continuous batching as single-process scheduling policies

Both are *request* policies in the literature, but each reduces to a small set of facts the
**kernel** scheduler must be able to see, and a small set of things it must be able to do.

### 13.2.1 What the scheduler must see

| Input | Source | Why |
|---|---|---|
| total token count `T` for the iteration | caller | the roofline coordinate (§13.1.1) |
| per-item `(l_qo, l_kv)` | KV manager | attention class + load balance (FlashInfer `α·l_q + β·l_kv`) |
| GQA group `g`, head dim `d`, head counts | model | effective query length `g·l_qo` |
| per-kernel **tile granularity** per dimension | compiler | 257 vs 256 costs **+32%** (Sarathi) |
| measured `T_crossover` / `L_m` per (kernel, device) | profiling pass | admission and co-run decisions |
| token budget `τ` from the latency target | policy | Sarathi: **512 strict, 2048 relaxed, 1536 for PP** |
| resident KV occupancy and free slots | KV pool | admission; deadlock avoidance (Orca) |
| chunk index / prior-chunk length sum | scheduler state | the `O(N²)` KV re-read term (DistServe) |
| whether an op is token-axis-flattenable | compiler | Orca's batching legality predicate |

### 13.2.2 What the scheduler must be able to do

1. **Build a launch from non-uniform work items.** Orca already did this in 2022 by "concatenating
   all thread blocks of the kernels for different requests… which is often discouraged by CUDA
   programming practice… we find this fusion to be beneficial by improving GPU utilization and
   reducing the kernel launch overhead." FlashInfer formalized it as a work queue of
   (query-tile, KV-chunk) items assigned to CTAs by a cost-balanced priority queue. **This is the
   single most important capability for the cajeta submit seam**: one launch, many geometries.
2. **Round every chunk to tile granularity.** Non-negotiable: +32% for a one-token miss.
3. **Hold a per-iteration cost budget.** Sarathi's `τ` is exactly a frame budget applied to a token
   count. The scheduler fills the budget decode-first, then partial prefills, then new work.
4. **Refuse to admit a whole prefill into a decode iteration.** Measured penalties for doing so:
   **up to 28.3×** TBT (LLaMA2-70B, ctx 4096, batch 1) and **20.3×** (Mistral-7B, ctx 4096,
   batch 1) versus **1.2–3.7×** for a chunked prefill. On a 13B DistServe measures **3–6×** for a
   *single* added prefill.
5. **Partition compute units, not just batches.** DistServe's interference numbers say that mixing
   phases into one launch inherits the compute-bound kernel's latency. FlashInfer's answer is that
   the SM count is a *plan input*: "this SM number can be provided by the user through the plan
   functions and the FlashInfer load-balancing scheduler will allocate tiles accordingly." The
   cajeta submit seam should carry a **compute-unit budget per launch**, and the tile planner must
   re-plan against it.

### 13.2.3 Numeric budgets to build into the spec

- Token budget `τ`: **512** (strict latency), **2048** (relaxed), **1536** where pipeline bubbles
  matter. Chunk overhead: **≤25% at chunk 512, ≈0 at chunk 2048** (Yi-34B, TP-2, prompts 2–8K).
- Prefill saturation cap: **~2048 prompt tokens** (Splitwise, BLOOM-176B/Llama2-70B on H100);
  **~512 tokens** (DistServe, 13B on one A100). Both are "the point where more batching stops
  helping" — measure per model+device, do not hard-code.
- Latency SLOs actually used, as a calibration for what "strict" means: Mistral-7B P99 TBT
  **0.1 s / 0.5 s**; Yi-34B **0.2 s / 1 s**; LLaMA2-70B and Falcon-180B **1 s / 5 s** — defined as
  5× and 25× the execution time of a decode iteration at 4K prefill, batch 32. DistServe's
  application SLOs: chatbot 13B TTFT 0.25 s / TPOT 0.1 s; code completion 66B 0.125 s / 0.2 s;
  summarization 66B 15 s / 0.15 s.
- Real workload shapes to test against: ShareGPT prompt median **1730** / P90 5696, output median
  **415**; arxiv-summarization prompt median **7059** / P90 12985, output median 208 (Sarathi).
  Azure production: coding prompt median **1500**, output median **13**; conversation prompt
  **1020**, output **129** (Splitwise). ShareGPT avg 755.5-in / 200.3-out, HumanEval 1713/98.2,
  LongBench 1738.3/90.7 (DistServe). **Prompt:output runs 4:1 to 115:1 — prefill is never a
  rounding error.**
- Real batch occupancy: **60–70% of the time fewer than 20 active tokens; >20% of the time a single
  token** (Splitwise, production traces). Design the decode path for `T ≤ 20`, not for `T = 64`.

## 13.3 KV-cache residency as a scheduler-visible resource

KV is unlike a launch's transient working set in four ways, all of which the submit seam's
read/write buffer sets cannot express today: it is **large**, **long-lived across many launches**,
**growing during the sequence**, and **shareable by reference count**.

**Sizes.** Per token, `KV_bytes = 2 · n_kv_heads · d_head · n_layers · b_kv`.
- OPT-13B (MHA, fp16): **800 KB/token** → a 2048-token request needs **1.6 GB** (vLLM).
- OPT-66B: a single 512-token request = **1.13 GB** (DistServe).
- A GQA-4 8B model (8 KV heads × 128, 32 layers, fp16): **128 KB/token** → 8K context ≈ **1 GB**
  (derived; this is the cajeta-llm-relevant regime).
- Budget on a 40 GB A100 with a 13B model: 26 GB weights, **12 GB KV = 15.7K slots**. 8×A100-80GB
  with 175B: 346 GB weights, **264 GB KV = 60.1K slots** (vLLM Table 1).

**Why it must be scheduler-visible, not allocator-visible:**
- **It sets the batch size, and therefore the roofline class.** vLLM raised the resident batch from
  7.00 → **30.42** (ShareGPT) and 7.00 → **132.44** (Alpaca) for OPT-13B purely by defragmenting.
  Splitwise: token-phase throughput "keeps increasing with batching until 64 batch-size, at which
  point the machine runs out of memory."
- **Naive reservation wastes 60–80%.** Effective utilization measured at **20.4%** (Orca-Max),
  26.8%, 38.2% (Orca-Oracle) vs **96.3%** (vLLM).
- **Admission must be checked, or the scheduler deadlocks.** Orca: reserve `max_tokens` slots at
  admission; "a naïve implementation can make the scheduler fall into a deadlock when the scheduler
  cannot issue an iteration for a new request… because there is no space left for storing a new
  Attention key and value."
- **Eviction is gang-structured.** vLLM's all-or-nothing per-sequence eviction, with sequence
  *groups* (beam candidates) gang-scheduled.
- **Recompute vs spill is a measured choice.** Recompute cost is constant in block size and "never
  higher than 20% of swapping's latency"; swapping wins only at large blocks; 16–64 is a wash.
- **Indirection is not free and is bounded to one kernel.** Paging costs **20–26% on the attention
  kernel only** — "the overhead is small as it only affects the attention operator but not the
  other operators in the model, such as Linear."
- **Block size couples the memory manager to the subgroup width.** vLLM assigns **one warp per
  block** and defaults to **block size 16**; 16–128 is fine for long sequences, but for short
  sequences (Alpaca) anything above 32 "significantly degrades" performance.
- **The scope warning matters for the multimodal mandate.** vLLM: "in DNN training, the tensor
  shapes are typically static… in serving DNNs that are not LLMs, an increase in memory efficiency
  may not result in any performance improvement since the performance is primarily compute-bound.
  In such scenarios, introducing vLLM's techniques may rather degrade the performance." **Paging is
  a per-workload policy, never a device-wide mechanism.**

**Requirement shape:** the cajeta seam needs a `ResidentPool` concept alongside buffer sets:
capacity, block size, occupancy, per-item reservation, per-item block list, refcounts, gang
membership, and a per-item recompute cost so eviction can be decided.

## 13.4 MoE routing's effect on kernel shape and load balance

MoE replaces one large dense GEMM with **one tiny router GEMM + a permutation + `E` ragged GEMMs +
an inverse permutation**. The scheduler-visible consequences:

- **Shape is data-dependent and known only at runtime, on-device.** The realized token count per
  expert `f_i·T` exists after the router and before the expert GEMMs. Switch's TPU implementation
  had to pad to a static **expert capacity** `C = (tokens_per_batch / E) × capacity_factor`; a
  single-process GPU scheduler does not, and should not — it should take the launch bound from the
  device-side count (indirect dispatch).
- **Capacity factor is a real throughput knob worth ~14%**: Switch-Base 1000 examples/s at CF 1.0
  vs 860 at CF 2.0 (32 TPUv3 cores, FLOP-matched). And it is **not monotone**: MoE-Base got
  *slower* going CF 2.0 → 1.25 (840 → 790). Measure, don't reason.
- **Dropped tokens are the imbalance metric**: **<1%** with the aux loss `α·N·Σf_i·P_i` at
  `α = 10⁻²`, independent of the number of experts. The kernel-level analogue is "fraction of a
  launch's work items that did not fit the planned tiles."
- **At decode batch sizes MoE is a launch-bound swarm.** With `T = 20` active tokens (Splitwise's
  measured typical), top-1 routing and `E = 64` experts, the mean expert sees **0.3 rows**. Each
  expert GEMM is `[~0–2, d] × [d, d_ff]` — a pure weight read, memory-bound and dominated by fixed
  overhead. **This is the worst launch-overhead case in the entire workload class** and is the
  strongest argument for (a) a grouped/batched GEMM primitive taking per-group offsets, and
  (b) co-scheduling, since none of these kernels can fill the device alone.
- **The dependency chain is strict per layer**: router → dispatch → experts → combine → next layer.
  Only the `E` expert launches are mutually parallel. Overlap must come from *other requests* or
  *other model stages*, not from within the layer.
- **Precision boundaries are placement constraints.** Switch's selective precision keeps the router
  in fp32 *locally* and casts back to bf16 before the all-to-all; doing it globally cost **1390 →
  1160 examples/s (≈17%)**. A fusion or retiling pass must not widen an fp32 region across a
  dispatch boundary.
- Routing cost itself is negligible: `O(d_model × E)`.

## 13.5 Speculative decoding's draft/verify overlap

- **The mechanism is a memory-to-compute conversion.** Leviathan states it directly: the target
  model's weights and KV "can be read **once per execution** of Algorithm 1, so the number of memory
  accesses for reading them shrinks by a factor of `(1 − α^{γ+1})/(1 − α)`", while total arithmetic
  rises by only `(1−α)(γĉ+γ+1)/(1−α^{γ+1})`. Measured trade at `c = ĉ = 0`: **1.11–1.63× more ops
  for 1.96–6.86× less wall time**.
- **Measured end-to-end**: T5-XXL (11B) with a T5-small (77M) draft on a **single TPU-v4**, batch 1:
  **3.4×** (EnDe, temp 0, γ=7, α=0.75), 2.6× (temp 1), **3.1×** (CNN/DM, temp 0, γ=5, α=0.65).
  Chinchilla 70B independently: 2–2.5×.
- **Within one request there is NO draft/verify overlap.** The draft chain is `γ` strictly serial
  steps, and the verify needs all `γ` guesses. Overlap exists only **across requests**: request A's
  verify (a mini-prefill, compute-heavy) against request B's draft chain (tiny, memory- and
  launch-bound). That is a textbook complementary pair.
- **The draft chain is the launch-overhead stress test.** `γ = 5–10` serial forward passes of a
  model "around two orders of magnitude smaller" than the target, at batch 1. Per accepted-token
  group that is hundreds of kernel launches for a few hundred microseconds of real work. **A
  capture/replay (graph) path or a persistent kernel for the draft chain is a prerequisite, not an
  optimization.**
- **The verify launch is a `γ+1`-query prefill.** With GQA `g = 8` and `γ = 7`, the effective query
  length is 64 — tensor-core tile territory — versus 8 for a plain decode step. `γ` alone changes
  the kernel's class, its tile size, and its parallel axis.
- **The committed work is data-dependent.** The accepted count `n` is known only post-verify;
  the KV commit and the next draft's start state are dynamic. Needs indirect launch bounds or a
  predicated worst-case launch.
- **`γ` must be tuned online**: optimal `γ` from `α` and the draft/target cost ratio `c` — at
  `c = 0.05`, α = 0.7 → γ ≈ 4; α = 0.9 → γ ≈ 10–12. Acceptance rates measured: 0.62–0.75 (T5-small
  draft for T5-XXL), 0.88–0.89 (6M draft for a 97M target), 0.57–0.75 (LaMDA 100M–8B drafts for
  137B), 0.19–0.20 (bigram).
- **The scope condition is the same predicate the scheduler already needs**: "our method is not
  helpful for configurations where additional computation resources are not available. However, in
  common cases where additional computation resources are available (e.g. when memory bandwidth is
  the bottleneck) our method provides the speedup." *So: any launch the scheduler has tagged
  memory-bound has, by construction, spare compute to speculate into — and §13.1.1's quantization
  result says that spare compute shrinks by ~4× for a Q4 model.*

## 13.6 What TTS/STT and CNN pipelines add — **[not from these papers; my own knowledge]**

**No paper in this set covers speech or vision.** The following is engineering knowledge, flagged
as such, and the numbers are order-of-magnitude, not citations.

### 13.6.1 STT / ASR

- **Whisper-class**: a conv front-end (2 × Conv1d, stride 2) over an 80-mel × 3000-frame (30 s)
  window, then a transformer *encoder* over 1500 states — this is a **prefill-shaped,
  compute-bound, single-shot launch** with fully static geometry. The *decoder* is autoregressive
  with **cross-attention over a fixed 1500-state KV that is computed once and never grows** — a
  third KV regime, distinct from the growing self-attention KV, and one that a resident-pool
  abstraction must model (static, shared, read-only for the whole utterance).
- **Streaming ASR** (conformer/RNN-T class): the encoder is chunked (typically 160–960 ms of audio,
  i.e. 16–96 frames at a 10 ms hop) with causal or chunked attention. Each chunk arrives on a
  **hard period** equal to its duration; the real-time factor must stay below 1 with margin. This
  is the workload that genuinely needs the **frame-budget** policy, and it is the only one in this
  document with true periodic deadlines.
- Kernel character: small, static, many. A conformer block at chunk 32 frames is a `[32, 512]`
  activation — deep in the launch-bound regime for every GEMM. Static shapes make graph capture
  trivially applicable, unlike LLM serving's ragged dynamism.

### 13.6.2 TTS

- Two stages with opposite characters. **(1) Acoustic model**: either autoregressive
  (Tacotron/VALL-E/Bark class, ~50–90 mel frames/s, decode-shaped, memory- and launch-bound) or
  diffusion/flow-matching (a *fixed* count of 20–50 denoising steps over the whole utterance, each
  a medium-size compute-bound U-Net/DiT launch — a predictable, schedulable block of compute with
  no data-dependent length). **(2) Vocoder** (HiFi-GAN class): transposed convolutions, memory-bound
  at streaming chunk sizes, embarrassingly parallel over time when batched.
- **Hard budget**: 24 kHz audio at a 256-sample hop = 93.75 frames/s = **10.7 ms per frame**.
  A conversational agent's mouth-to-ear budget is ~200–300 ms end-to-end, which must cover
  STT + LLM decode + TTS. Since the LLM decode in this stack is itself ~12 ms/token (cajeta-llm on
  gfx1151), the three pipelines *must* share one GPU under a deadline policy rather than each
  owning it.
- Scheduling shape: TTS and STT are **producer/consumer with periodic deadlines**, the LLM is
  **best-effort throughput**. Co-running them is the frame-budget policy's reason to exist:
  the LLM's memory-bound decode is exactly the workload that can be preempted or throttled to let a
  10.7 ms vocoder frame land, and the vocoder is exactly the compute-bound work that fills the
  decode's idle ALUs.

### 13.6.3 CNN vision

- **Convolutions are compute-bound at training batch sizes and often memory-/launch-bound at
  batch 1 inference.** A ResNet-50 forward at batch 1 is ~50 conv launches of tens to a couple of
  hundred microseconds; **depthwise-separable** convs (MobileNet/EfficientNet class) are memory-bound
  nearly everywhere, because a depthwise kernel does ~`k²` MACs per weight element with almost no
  reuse.
- **ViT-style vision encoders are prefill-shaped**: one transformer forward over 196–1024 patch
  tokens, compute-bound, single-shot, static geometry — the same class as an LLM prefill chunk, and
  therefore the same complementary partner for LLM decode.
- What vision adds to the requirements: (i) **NCHW/NHWC layout and im2col/implicit-GEMM choice** is
  a per-launch tiling decision with the same tile-quantization sensitivity as the LLM GEMMs;
  (ii) **static shapes** ⇒ the whole graph can be planned once; (iii) at batch 1 the pipeline is
  dominated by fixed overhead, so **fusion and capture matter more than tiling**.

### 13.6.4 The common additions

Speech and vision pipelines add four things the LLM papers do not exercise:
1. **Hard periodic deadlines** (10.7 ms audio frames, 16.7/33.3 ms video frames) → the
   frame-budget policy needs real preemption or at least admission that respects a deadline.
2. **Many small launches with fixed, statically-known shapes** → launch overhead is first-order and
   capture/replay is cheap to apply. **[my own knowledge]** an un-captured HIP/CUDA launch costs
   ~3–10 µs end-to-end with ~2–5 µs of unavoidable gap; graph replay drops per-node overhead to
   ~0.5–2 µs. Against a 10.7 ms frame budget, 300 un-captured launches is ~1–3 ms — 10–30% of the
   budget spent on nothing.
3. **A third KV/state regime**: static cross-attention KV (ASR decoder), and streaming ring buffers
   (conformer left-context, vocoder overlap-add state) that are neither transient nor growing.
4. **Multiple concurrent models in one process** — STT, LLM, TTS, and a vision encoder are four
   distinct weight sets resident at once. The residency accounting in §13.3 must cover weights as
   well as KV.

## 13.7 What training adds

### 13.7.1 The backward pass

- **~2.5× the forward FLOPs**: FA2 counts **5 matmuls in the backward vs 2 in the forward**.
  Forward attention FLOPs `= 4·seqlen²·head_dim·num_heads` (halved for causal).
- **Different parallel axis and different sharing.** FA2 parallelizes the forward over *row* blocks
  with no cross-block communication (embarrassingly parallel, freely splittable), and the backward
  over *column* blocks **with atomic accumulation into `dQ`**. Consequence for the submit seam:
  **the write set must distinguish exclusive-write from accumulating-write**; two launches that
  accumulate into the same buffer cannot co-run, and the result is order-dependent in float.
- **Fusion legality changes.** FlashAttention: "in the context of model training, the intermediate
  values still need to be written to HBM to save for the backward pass, reducing the effectiveness
  of naive kernel fusion." The survey says the same from the other side: "since the backward
  computation is not required for LLM inference, more kernel fusion chances exist." **The compiler
  must tell the scheduler whether an intermediate is live into a backward pass.**
- **Recomputation is the standard answer and it is a real scheduled launch.** FlashAttention
  recomputes `S`, `P` on-chip from `(Q,K,V,m,ℓ)`; Megatron applies **activation checkpointing after
  every transformer layer**. Both trade FLOPs for residency, and both must appear in the schedule
  with their own cost and buffer reservation — never as free.

### 13.7.2 The optimizer step

**[not from these papers; my own knowledge, flagged]** Adam is pure elementwise streaming over every
parameter: read `p, g, m, v`, write `p, m, v`. In fp32 that is 16 bytes of state per parameter and
~24 bytes of traffic per parameter per step. For a 1B-parameter model: **~24 GB of traffic per
step**, which at 200 GB/s is **~120 ms** — comparable to or larger than a small model's whole
forward+backward. It is **unconditionally memory-bound**, it has **no dependencies among
parameters**, and if issued naively it is *thousands of tiny launches* (one per tensor). Two
requirements follow: it must be expressible as **one fused multi-tensor launch**, and it is the
ideal co-run partner for anything compute-bound.

### 13.7.3 Gradient reduction overlap — Megatron's rules

- **Launch each parameter's reduction as soon as its gradient is complete**, not at the end of the
  backward: "During back propagation we run **multiple gradient all-reduces in parallel** to reduce
  weight gradients within each distinct data parallel group." The scheduler must track
  **per-parameter gradient readiness** and be able to issue a reduction/transfer on a queue that
  runs concurrently with the next backward kernel.
- **Choose partition axes so no synchronization is needed between fused GEMMs.** Megatron splits the
  MLP's first weight **column-wise** precisely so the elementwise GeLU needs no cross-partition sum,
  then splits the second **row-wise** so it consumes the local output — one all-reduce forward, one
  backward, **4 collectives per transformer layer per fwd+bwd**. Same reasoning as FA2's choice to
  split Q rather than K,V across warps: *which operand axis is partitioned determines whether the
  next op needs a cross-group reduction.*
- **Duplicate cheap memory-bound work rather than communicate it**: "Rather than having one GPU
  compute part of the dropout, layer normalization, or residual connections and broadcast the
  results to other GPUs, we choose to **duplicate the computation across GPUs**." Generalized
  in-process: *recompute a memory-bound elementwise op instead of synchronizing around it.*
- **Shrink the collective's payload by fusing it with its producer**: the logit GEMM fused with
  cross-entropy turns a `b×s×v` all-gather (v = 51,200) into a `b×s` all-reduce.
- **Pad to tile granularity**: "it is beneficial for the per-GPU vocabulary size to be a multiple of
  **128**", hence 50,257 → 51,200. Same class of constraint as Sarathi's 257-vs-256 +32%.
- **RNG stream identity is a correctness property**: dropout inside a model-parallel region must
  draw different numbers per worker; dropout outside must draw identical ones. Reordering launches
  must not change which stream a kernel draws from.

### 13.7.4 Realistic training efficiency targets

- Megatron single-V100 baseline: **39 TFLOPs = 30% of peak**, called "a strong single GPU baseline."
- FA2 end-to-end GPT training on 8×A100-80GB: **196–225 TFLOPs/s per GPU = up to 72% MFU**, and the
  long-context row is the telling one — at **8K context the un-optimized baseline collapses from
  142 → 72 TFLOPs/s while FA2 rises from 196 → 220**.
- FA2 attention kernel alone: **50–73% of A100 peak** forward, up to 63% backward; FA1 was 30–50%
  and 25–35%; a tuned GEMM is 80–90%. **A100 non-matmul FP32 peak is 19.5 TFLOPs/s vs 312 for
  fp16 matmul — one non-matmul FLOP costs ~16 matmul FLOPs.**

## 13.8 Consolidated submit-seam metadata (what every launch must carry)

Derived from all twelve papers. Items marked ★ are ones a naive design would omit and that a
measurement in this document proves are needed.

**Geometry**
- work-item list with **per-item geometry** (not one uniform shape) ★ — Orca, FlashInfer
- total token count `T` (the roofline coordinate) — Sarathi, DistServe
- per-item `(l_qo, l_kv)`, head counts `H_qo/H_kv` (⇒ GQA group `g`), head dim `d` — FlashInfer
- chosen tile shape and the **tile granularity per dimension** ★ — Sarathi (+32%), Megatron (128)
- implied workgroup count, to compare against the CU count ★ — FA2 ("≥80" on 108 SMs)
- per-group register + LDS footprint, checked against the target's budget ★ — FA1 (`M`), FA2
  ("the kernel cannot run at all")
- mask pattern (dense / causal / block-sparse / tree) and its per-row nonzero counts ★ — FA2, FlashInfer

**Buffers**
- read set, **exclusive**-write set, and **accumulating**-write set as distinct categories ★ — FA2 (`dQ` atomics)
- resident-pool handles with occupancy, block size, per-item reservation, refcounts, gang membership ★ — vLLM, Orca
- per-region (per-layer) output slices so consumers can be released incrementally ★ — Splitwise
- workspace section base + capacity, stable under capture ★ — FlashInfer
- indirect (device-side) launch bounds for data-dependent work counts ★ — MoE, speculative decoding
- whether an intermediate is live into a backward pass ★ — FA1, survey

**Arithmetic character** (not one bit ★)
- matrix-core FLOPs, vector-ALU FLOPs, and bytes, **separately** — FA2's 16:1 ratio
- stored bit width of weights and activations (bytes moved ≠ compute width) — survey, §13.1.1
- derived class = `memory | compute | launch-bound`, computed against the **measured** per-device
  `R` and `T_crossover`, never asserted by the author

**Policy / control**
- schedule policy: throughput | latency | frame-budget, with a deadline for the last two
- a **compute-unit budget** for this launch ★ — FlashInfer/Nanoflow SM partition, DistServe interference
- a per-iteration cost budget `τ` — Sarathi
- power class (does this launch push toward TDP?) ★ — Splitwise
- RNG stream identity ★ — Megatron
- phase tag (initial / incremental) and a forward/backward tag — survey, FA2
- a capture/replay identity so a repeated launch sequence submits once ★ — FlashInfer, speculative
  decoding's draft chain

## 13.9 The ten requirements the whole corpus agrees on

1. **When a launch is classified, the class must be derived from `(FLOPs, bytes, T, l_qo, g,
   b_w)` against a measured per-device `R`** — never declared by the kernel author, never per
   operator. The same attention operator spans ~400× in intensity, and the same GEMM flips class at
   `T_crossover`.
2. **When two runnable launches have opposite classes, prefer to co-run them; when they have the
   same class, serialize.** (Splitwise's phase characterization; Sarathi's free-compute argument.)
3. **When two launches are co-run, they must be granted disjoint compute-unit sets and the tile
   plan must be recomputed against the grant** — sharing a batch instead costs 3–6× (DistServe) up
   to 28.3× (Sarathi).
4. **When a launch's work items are non-uniform, it must still be ONE launch**, with a cost-balanced
   assignment of items to compute units (`α·l_q + β·l_kv`). Skewed-length workloads lose **38% of
   ITL** to bad balance alone (FlashInfer).
5. **When any geometry is chosen, it must be a multiple of the kernel's tile granularity.**
   257 vs 256 = **+32%** (Sarathi); vocab padded to 128× (Megatron).
6. **When the implied workgroup count is below the device's CU count, add a reduction axis
   (split-K / KV-chunk) before launching** — FA2's "≥80 on 108 SMs", FlashDecoding, FlashInfer's
   split-K writethrough.
7. **When long-lived state (KV, weights, streaming ring buffers) is required, it must be modelled
   as a resident pool with occupancy and admission**, with gang-structured eviction and a
   recompute-vs-spill decision driven by measured costs.
8. **When work is data-dependent (MoE expert counts, accepted speculative tokens), the launch bound
   must come from a device buffer**, not a host constant or a padded worst case.
9. **When a repeated launch sequence has stable geometry and stable buffer addresses, it must be
   captured once and replayed** — mandatory for MoE decode swarms, speculative draft chains, and
   every streaming speech/vision pipeline.
10. **When any of the above is changed, the effect must be measurable through the seam**: achieved
    bandwidth, achieved fraction of matrix-core peak, workgroups launched, spill/LDS occupancy, and
    the realized per-item balance. Every paper here found its result by measuring one of these, and
    three of them (FA1→FA2, FlashInfer's LB ablation, Switch's capacity factor) found that the
    obvious reasoning was wrong.
